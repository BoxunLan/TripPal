Quick answer
For most travelers, a realistic street food meal in China costs ¥15-35 . A small breakfast can be ¥5-18 , a bowl of noodles or rice noodles is usually ¥10-30 , dumplings or pan-fried snacks are often ¥8-30 , and a casual skewer or malatang meal can reach ¥20-60 . Hotpot is not street food; treat it as a sit-down meal and budget ¥70-150+ per person. A sit-down restaurant meal costs more: in MyChinaGuide's restaurant data, the median spend per person runs from ¥55 in Xi'an to ¥120 in Shanghai.
Snack prices below are typical ranges, not quotes from one shop; check the posted price before you order.
The old idea that every Chinese street snack costs ¥5 is out of date. You can still find ¥3 baozi and ¥6 soy milk. You can also spend ¥45 on a tourist-street jianbing with sausage, cheese, and a glossy menu. Both happen. The useful question is not "Is China cheap?" It is: what kind of snack are you ordering, and where are you standing when you order it?
This guide breaks prices down by street food type first, then adjusts for cities like Beijing, Shanghai, Chengdu, Xi'an, Chongqing, and Kunming.

What counts as a street food meal in China
In China, "street food" is not only a cart on the sidewalk. Many local meals that foreigners call street food are sold from tiny shops with one counter, a few stools, and a QR code taped near the cashier.
For this price guide, street food includes:
Breakfast carts and small breakfast shops

Noodle, rice noodle, wonton, and dumpling counters

Baozi, jianbing, shengjian, guotie, and other quick snacks

Skewer stalls, chuan chuan shops, barbecue stands, and late-night food streets

Malatang, boboji, liangpi, bingfen, milk tea, and fruit tea shops

It does not include full-service restaurants, premium hotpot chains, Peking duck restaurants, hotel dining, airport food courts, or themed tourist streets where the main product is the experience, not the snack.

A normal local breakfast might be two baozi and soy milk for ¥8-15 , or one jianbing with egg and crispy cracker for ¥8-18 . Add sausage, extra egg, cheese, or a tourist-area location, and the same breakfast can move toward ¥20-30 .
City notes:
Beijing: jianbing near subway exits is usually reasonable; landmark food streets cost more.

Shanghai: cifan, shengjian, and coffee-shop breakfast combos can raise the morning budget.

Guangzhou/Shenzhen: changfen can be cheap in neighborhood shops but higher in polished dim sum spaces.

Xi'an/Chengdu/Chongqing: breakfast stays good value if you avoid scenic-area streets.

For a traveler, the easiest breakfast order is: point to what locals are buying, say yi ge ("one"), and pay by QR code.

This is where ¥25 is still a useful benchmark. In Chengdu, Chongqing, Xi'an, Kunming, Nanning, Lanzhou-style noodle shops, and many residential neighborhoods, ¥25 is enough for a real bowl. In Shanghai or Beijing malls, the same category often moves to ¥28-45 .
Do not judge a noodle shop only by the English name. A small shop with a handwritten menu and a busy lunch line often beats a polished chain on value. Chains are still useful for foreigners because they usually have photos, digital menus, cleaner seating, and predictable payment.

Dumplings, buns and pan-fried snacks: shengjian, xiaolongbao, guotie

Shanghai is the obvious city for shengjian and xiaolongbao, but it is also where the price range stretches the most. A local counter can be reasonable. A famous chain or tourist-heavy old street can cost much more. Xi'an is better value for roujiamo and other wheat-based snacks.
One practical rule: if a snack is sold by piece, count how many pieces make a meal. A ¥6 baozi is cheap, but you may need two or three. A ¥18 roujiamo can be a full lunch. Four ¥16 shengjian are a snack; eight plus soup become a meal.
Skewers, chuan chuan and late-night barbecue
Skewers look cheap because the unit price is small. The bill depends on how many sticks you eat, whether meat is priced differently, and whether the place adds a soup base, dipping sauce, or table fee.

In Chengdu and Chongqing, chuan chuan can be excellent value if you eat lightly. It can also stop being cheap once you add beef, tripe, duck intestine, beer, and multiple dipping sauces. In northern cities, lamb skewers are often the late-night classic. In Beijing or tourist-heavy areas, the same stick can cost noticeably more than in a residential neighborhood.
If you are on a tight budget, order slowly. Do not load the whole basket before you know the pricing system.

Malatang and boboji are priced by weight, stick, or bowl depending on the shop. This is where travelers accidentally overspend: vegetables look light, meatballs and tofu products add up, and some shops have a minimum charge.
Before you start choosing ingredients, look for:
Whether the price is by jin (500g), by bowl, or by stick

Whether meat and vegetables have different prices

Whether rice, dipping sauce, or soup base is included

Whether the shop has a minimum spend

For most travelers, ¥25-40 is a normal malatang meal. If you build a huge bowl, expect more.
Chains such as Yang Guo Fu and Zhang Liang charge by weight, and each shop sets its own rate. Stores reported in November 2025 charged ¥25.8-28.8 per jin (500g), with meat and vegetables at the same price in some of them. Some shops post the price per 50g, which looks small until you multiply it: ¥2.88 per 50g is ¥28.8 per jin. You pay for the raw ingredients before they are cooked, and items that hold water, such as tofu and fresh noodles, weigh more than they look. Check the weight and the total on the scale before you pay.

The same snack type can jump because of rent, queue fame, or a famous location. A local jianbing near a residential compound and a jianbing next to a landmark are not competing in the same market.
If you want better prices, walk away from the landmark first. Two side streets can be enough.
How to avoid tourist prices
You do not need to hunt for secret stalls. You just need to avoid the obvious traps.
Better signs:
Locals are eating there before work or during lunch

Menu is Chinese-first, even if photos help

Prices are posted clearly

Food is cooked hot and turns over quickly

Staff are busy rather than calling people in

The shop is near homes, offices, schools, or a wet market

Weaker signs:
English-only menu

Staff standing outside pulling tourists in

Big laminated photo board with every "famous Chinese snack"

Location directly beside a landmark exit

Food sitting under heat lamps for a long time

Prices that feel rounded for tourists rather than normal local pricing

The simplest move: do not eat your main meal on the most famous snack street in the city. Go there to look, try one thing if you want, then eat dinner somewhere local.

Payment and ordering tips
Most street-food-style places now expect QR payment. Foreigners can usually manage this with Alipay or WeChat Pay linked to an eligible international card, but do not assume every card or every merchant will work every time.
Practical setup:
Set up Alipay before arrival and bind an eligible international card.

Set up WeChat Pay as a backup if possible.

Keep small cash notes: ¥5, ¥10, ¥20, ¥50.

Do not rely on a ¥100 note at a tiny breakfast stall.

If payment fails, try another app, another card, or ask whether cash is okay.

If the menu is only in Chinese, use camera translation, point at another customer's order, or choose a place with photos. Most small shops are used to quick orders and will not expect a conversation.
For payment setup, read our Alipay for foreigners guide .
Foreign-card fees rarely matter for street food. WeChat Pay charges no fee on payments of ¥200 or less and 3% of the whole payment above that, and Alipay uses the same ¥200 line. A snack or a noodle bowl stays under it; a group dinner paid in one go may not.
Cash is still legal tender. Since February 1, 2026, a central bank rule has barred businesses that take payment in person from refusing it, so small notes are a valid backup when a QR payment fails.

FAQ
Is ¥25 enough for a street food meal in China?
Yes, in many cities ¥25 is enough for one simple street-food-style meal, especially a bowl of noodles, rice noodles, dumplings, baozi, jianbing, malatang, or a few skewers. In Shanghai, Beijing tourist areas, airport areas, and sit-down restaurants, ¥25 may only cover a small snack.
What is the cheapest type of street food in China?
Breakfast items are usually the cheapest: baozi, youtiao, soy milk, jianbing, rice rolls, and sticky rice rolls commonly sit in the ¥3-18 range depending on the city and toppings.
Is hotpot considered street food in China?
No. Hotpot is better treated as a sit-down meal. It can be casual and local, but the usual per-person cost is closer to ¥70-150+ than to a quick street snack.
Can foreigners pay for street food in China?
Usually yes, but the easiest path is Alipay or WeChat Pay with an eligible international card. Keep small cash notes as a backup because tiny stalls may not have change for large bills.
Are tourist food streets in China overpriced?
They can be. Food streets near major landmarks often charge more because of rent and tourist traffic. Walk into neighborhood lanes, morning markets, or areas near residential compounds for more normal local prices.
How much should I budget per day for street food in China?
For a street-food-heavy day, budget around ¥50-90 in Chengdu, Xi'an, Chongqing, Kunming, and similar cities; ¥80-140 in Beijing and Shanghai if you avoid landmark tourist streets; and more if you add hotpot, Peking duck, cocktails, or restaurant meals.
Do foreign cards work for street food, and do I pay a fee?
Usually. Once your foreign Visa or Mastercard is linked to Alipay or WeChat Pay, you scan the vendor's QR code like a local. There is no 3% foreign-card fee on transactions of ¥200 or less — and almost no street food meal exceeds that — so in practice you pay zero fees on snacks.
Why did my malatang cost ¥50 when it looked cheap?
Most malatang chains charge by weight, and each shop sets its own rate; stores reported in late 2025 charged about ¥26-29 per jin (500g). Some post the price per 50g, which looks cheap until you multiply it by ten. Items that hold water, such as tofu and fresh noodles, weigh more than they look, so check the total on the scale before you pay.
How much does an average meal cost in China?
A quick street food meal costs about ¥15-35. At sit-down restaurants, the median spend per person in MyChinaGuide's restaurant database runs from ¥55 in Xi'an to ¥120 in Shanghai, and 10 of the 13 cities with at least 40 listings fall between ¥67 and ¥93. The database covers 1,025 restaurants in 22 cities and was last updated in June 2026.
Is food cheap in China for tourists?
Mostly, yes. A street food meal of ¥15-35 is about $2-5, and the median sit-down restaurant meal in MyChinaGuide's data is ¥55-120 per person, about $8-18, depending on the city (September 2026 exchange rate). Shanghai and Beijing have the highest restaurant medians, and landmark food streets, hotels, and airports charge more than neighborhood places.
