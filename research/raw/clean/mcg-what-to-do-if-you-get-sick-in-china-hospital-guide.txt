A fever in a hotel room in Shanghai at 2 in the morning is a different kind of panic than a fever at home. You do not know where to go. Your phone's translator is struggling with medical terms. The hotel front desk speaks some English, but not enough to explain cardiovascular risk factors. You are trying to decide: does this need an ambulance, or can it wait until morning?
The good news is that getting medical care in China in 2026 is a lot more foreigner-friendly than it was a decade ago. Major cities have both private international hospitals and public hospital "international departments" (国际部 guójìbù) that handle foreign patients daily. The bad news is that care is paid for upfront, insurance direct billing is rare, and navigating the system without a few basics explained to you in advance is genuinely stressful when you are already feeling awful.
This guide is the thing to read before you need it.

What to bring with you to any hospital visit

First, the decision: hospital or pharmacy?
Not every illness needs a hospital. Many travelers arrive in China with a stomach bug or a sinus cold that clears up on its own with rest and basic medication. A Chinese 药店 (yào diàn, pharmacy) can handle these without any doctor visit, and many everyday medicines that need a prescription in the US — painkillers, antihistamines, anti-diarrheals — sit on open shelves here. Antibiotics are the big exception: by law they are prescription-only (more on that below).
Pharmacy is fine for:
Mild fever, cold symptoms, sore throat, congestion

Mild food poisoning or traveler's diarrhea (day 1 to 2, manageable)

Headache, muscle ache, menstrual cramps

Minor allergic reactions, mild skin rashes

Cuts, scrapes, bruises, small burns

Pink eye, mild eye irritation

Insomnia or jet lag

Hospital is needed for:
Fever above 39°C (102.2°F) lasting more than 24 hours

Severe or bloody diarrhea, dehydration

Chest pain, difficulty breathing, rapid heart rate

Severe abdominal pain, especially lower right (appendicitis risk)

Severe allergic reaction (swelling, breathing issues)

Significant injury, suspected fracture, deep cut

Pregnancy complications

Mental health crisis

Anything involving loss of consciousness

Call 120 (ambulance) for:
Stroke symptoms (face drooping, arm weakness, speech trouble)

Heart attack symptoms (crushing chest pain, radiating to arm or jaw)

Severe breathing difficulty

Major trauma or accident

Anaphylaxis

Unconscious person

120 is the national ambulance number in China, the equivalent of 911 for medical emergencies. Call it like you would call an ambulance anywhere. In Tier 1 cities the dispatcher often has English-capable staff; in smaller cities you may need to use a translation app or get hotel staff to speak for you. The ambulance takes you to the nearest public hospital emergency room, which may not be an international-friendly one. You can request a specific hospital, but if your condition is urgent they go to the closest.
Private international hospitals versus public hospital international wings
China has two parallel systems for foreign patients, and understanding the difference matters for both cost and experience.
Private international hospitals
These are standalone hospitals run by international healthcare groups, operating in English, with Western-trained doctors, and charging rates similar to premium private care in Singapore or Hong Kong.
The main players in 2026:
Beijing United Family Hospital (BJU) — Flagship private international hospital in Beijing's Lido area. Full ER, maternity, surgery. Outpatient ¥1200 to ¥2000, ER starts at ¥2500.

Parkway Health (Shanghai) — Multiple clinics across Shanghai including Jinmao Tower, Hongqiao, and Pudong. Outpatient ¥1500 to ¥2500. 24-hour urgent care.

Raffles Medical — Singapore healthcare brand with clinics in Shanghai, Beijing, Chongqing, and Tianjin. Similar pricing to Parkway.

United Family Healthcare network — Beyond Beijing, UFH operates in Shanghai, Guangzhou, Tianjin, Qingdao, and Bo'ao.

International SOS clinics — Travel emergency providers with outpatient clinics in Beijing, Shanghai, Nanjing, and Tianjin. Outpatient ¥1000 to ¥1800.

These hospitals arrange interpreters if needed, bill a limited list of international insurers directly (Bupa, Cigna Global, Aetna International), and handle the whole visit in English.
Public hospital international wings (国际部)
Major public hospitals in tier-1 cities have established international departments for foreign patients. These operate inside the public hospital building (or sometimes a dedicated wing) and offer the same medical expertise as the regular hospital, but with English-speaking staff, faster registration, nicer waiting rooms, and passport-based registration instead of the Chinese ID system.
The key advantage: cost. International wings of public hospitals charge 1.5 to 3 times the regular public rate, but are still 50 to 70 percent cheaper than private international hospitals. For a straightforward fever or gastro issue, you can pay ¥300 to ¥800 at a public hospital international wing versus ¥1500+ at a private international hospital for the same consultation.
Well-regarded public hospital international wings include:
Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMC) International Medical Services — Beijing's most prestigious medical institution. International wing has English-speaking doctors and accepts passport registration.

Beijing Anzhen Hospital International Medical Service — Cardiology specialty, Beijing.

Shanghai Huashan Hospital International Medical Center — Top general hospital in Shanghai with international services.

Shanghai Ruijin Hospital Luwan Branch International — Another Shanghai flagship.

Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital International Wing — Guangzhou.

West China Hospital (Chengdu) — Massive medical center with an international wing handling southwest China foreign patients.

The experience at a public hospital international wing is cleaner and more efficient than the regular outpatient areas, which can feel overwhelming with hundreds of patients in crowded waiting rooms. But it is still inside a public hospital, so standards of bedside manner and decor are functional rather than hospitality-focused.

Everything is paid upfront. This is the single most important thing to understand about Chinese hospital billing. Before you see a doctor, you pay the registration fee. After the doctor orders tests, you pay the test fees at a cashier window before the tests happen. If admitted, you pay an admission deposit before a bed is assigned. At discharge, final bills are settled and any deposit remainder refunded.
Private international hospitals operate the same way, just with better cashier experiences. You will not walk out and get a bill in the mail.
Public hospital, paid out of pocket without insurance (2026 typical ranges): general outpatient registration ¥10–50; specialist registration ¥50–200; senior expert ¥200–500; emergency registration ¥50–150. Common tests: full blood count ¥30–80; liver/kidney panel ¥80–200; chest X-ray ¥50–150; CT scan ¥400–1,200; MRI ¥600–2,000. A 7-day course of common antibiotics runs ¥20–80. By contrast, a single GP visit at a private international clinic is ¥800–2,000 and a specialist ¥1,500–4,000 — roughly 20–40x the public price for the same illness. The gap is service and English, not medical quality: top public hospitals (三甲, Grade-A tertiary) employ the country's best specialists.
Calling 120 is not free and you pay afterward; pricing is set city by city, not nationally. In Beijing the pre-hospital emergency fee is ¥40, the vehicle fee is ¥50 for the first 3 km then ¥7 per additional km, plus ¥120 per transfer; waiting under 4 hours is free. In Shanghai the pre-hospital fee is ¥60, the vehicle fee is ¥30 plus ¥7 per km from the fourth kilometre, and waiting is ¥80 per hour. A typical in-city ride therefore costs roughly ¥150–400 — a fraction of Western ambulance bills. You pay at the hospital after arrival, not at the scene.
Payment methods that work
Cash (RMB) — Always accepted. Bring enough for outpatient visits (¥500 to ¥2000 per visit). ATMs work inside most big hospitals.

WeChat Pay and Alipay — Universal at all hospital cashiers in 2026.

Foreign credit card (Visa, Mastercard, Amex) — Accepted at private international hospitals and most public hospital international wings. Not accepted at regular public hospital cashiers.

Insurance direct billing — Only at private international hospitals, and only if your insurance is on their partner list. Always confirm before treatment.

If your insurance is not on the direct-billing list, you pay cash or card and submit the receipts to your insurer afterwards for reimbursement. Keep every single receipt, every prescription slip, and every diagnosis sheet. Photograph them on your phone before you leave the hospital.
What to bring to the hospital
Your passport. This is your ID in China; no substitute works. Hospitals require it for registration.

Cash (¥2000 to ¥5000 depending on severity).

Your travel insurance card and policy number.

Your phone with a translation app (Pleco, Google Translate with Chinese downloaded, or DeepL).

A list of any medications you take regularly, with generic names if possible (Chinese doctors recognize generic names better than brand names).

Known allergies written down in both English and Chinese if possible. Your hotel concierge can translate for you before you leave.

A friend or hotel staff member if possible. Even at international-friendly facilities, having someone who speaks Chinese makes registration and navigation much faster.

The basics of a Chinese hospital visit
Here is what a typical outpatient visit at a public hospital international wing looks like, start to finish. Private international hospitals simplify steps 2, 3, and 5 but the core flow is the same.
Registration (挂号 guàhào). Go to the international services desk or registration window. Show your passport. Pay the consultation fee (¥300 to ¥800 at public international, ¥1500+ at private). You receive a registration slip with a number.

Waiting and consultation. Wait for your number. At the international wing, wait times are typically 10 to 45 minutes. You see a doctor, often in a small consultation room. Bring your phone for translation if needed.

Tests, if ordered. The doctor writes test orders on slips. You take the slips to a cashier window, pay for the tests, then go to the lab or imaging department to have them done. Lab results often come back within 30 to 60 minutes.

Follow-up consultation. Return to the same doctor (or a replacement doctor at the international wing) to discuss results and receive a prescription.

Pharmacy. Take the prescription to the hospital pharmacy. Pay and receive medication. Hospital pharmacies dispense exactly what the doctor prescribed; quantity is usually a 3 to 7 day supply.

Discharge and receipts. Gather all receipts for insurance. Request a diagnosis summary (诊断证明 zhěnduàn zhèngmíng) in English if you want one for insurance; most international wings can provide this for free or for a small fee.

Pharmacies in China: what to know
Chinese pharmacies (药店 yào diàn) are everywhere. Major chains include YiFeng (益丰), LBX Pharmacy (老百姓大药房), and Dacheng (大参林). You will find one every few blocks in any major city. Most are open 8:00 to 22:00, some 24 hours in central areas.
What you can buy over the counter without a prescription that would require one in the US:
Ibuprofen (布洛芬) — ¥10 to ¥30 per box

Paracetamol (对乙酰氨基酚) — ¥5 to ¥15

Antihistamines like Cetirizine (西替利嗪) or Loratadine (氯雷他定) — ¥15 to ¥40

Antibiotics — prescription only — Not over the counter. Chinese law classifies antibiotics as strictly prescription-only, and since the 2024 drug-retail rules pharmacies can be fined for selling them without a prescription. Enforcement is uneven and a small pharmacy may still hand them over, but that is illegal, unreliable, and unsafe — the wrong antibiotic or dose does real harm. If you think you need antibiotics, see a doctor at a hospital outpatient clinic (门诊, you only need your passport), get a proper prescription, and fill it at the hospital pharmacy.

Anti-diarrheal medication (洛哌丁胺, loperamide) — ¥15 to ¥30

Cough syrup and decongestants — Wide range available

Motion sickness pills — ¥10 to ¥20

Basic wound care (bandages, antiseptic) — ¥5 to ¥30

What you cannot easily get without a doctor's visit:
Controlled substances (opioids, benzodiazepines, ADHD meds)

Insulin without a prescription (bring your own)

Most psychiatric medications

Specialty medications for chronic conditions

If you take regular prescription medication, bring your full supply from home plus a 10 to 14 day buffer for delays. Pharmacies cannot always substitute US-brand meds with Chinese equivalents.
A foreign prescription is not valid at a Chinese pharmacy — antibiotics, asthma inhalers, and chronic medications like blood-pressure drugs legally require a new prescription written on-site by a China-licensed doctor. In practice enforcement varies and some pharmacies still sell antibiotics over the counter, but do not count on it; for anything regulated, see a doctor first (the registration fee is only ¥10–50). Pharmacies are common, often open late, and staff rarely speak English, so show the generic drug name or a photo of your medication's box rather than a brand name from home.
Travel insurance: the reality
Most foreign travel insurance policies (World Nomads, Allianz, SafetyWing, Axa, Cigna, Bupa) technically cover China. The practical question is whether they direct-bill.
Direct billing is rare. Only a handful of private international hospitals accept direct billing from specific international insurers, and the list is short: Bupa, Cigna Global, Aetna International, Allianz Worldwide Care. Most other policies work on a reimbursement basis.
Reimbursement is normal but slow. You pay upfront, keep all receipts, submit a claim with the diagnosis summary and itemized bills, and wait 2 to 8 weeks for payment. Some insurers want receipts translated to English; most of the international hospitals will provide bilingual receipts on request.
What to do before you travel:
Check whether your policy covers China specifically (most do but confirm).

Note the 24-hour emergency phone number on the back of your card.

Understand your deductible and coverage limits.

Know whether you need to call the insurer before seeking non-emergency care.

Save a photo of your policy document on your phone.

Some policies (especially annual travel insurance) require you to call before any non-emergency treatment. Skipping this call can void coverage. Check your fine print.
For anything beyond outpatient — admission, surgery, observation — public hospitals take a deposit upfront, usually estimated at 30–50% of the projected total bill, not a fixed figure. This deposit is not a one-time hold: the hospital's system deducts ward fees, medication and consumables from it every night. If your balance drops below the next day's estimated cost, you get a top-up warning and non-emergency orders (scheduled scans, elective procedures) can be paused until you add funds. At discharge a final settlement is done and any unused deposit is refunded to the exact card or wallet you paid with. Emergency, life-saving treatment is never withheld over a low balance.
You do not need a WeChat or a Chinese bank account to get treated. Two reliable routes if you avoid WeChat: (1) Walk in and use the staffed registration window (挂号) or a self-service kiosk with your passport — pay the registration fee in cash or by tapping a foreign Visa/Mastercard, then carry the printed order slips between the cashier, lab, and pharmacy. (2) Link your foreign Visa, Mastercard, JCB, or Discover card to Alipay (verified by scanning your passport, usually under 10 minutes) and pay in-app for registration, tests, and the pharmacy. Major hospital international departments increasingly accept foreign physical cards at their POS terminals; standard outpatient desks may not, so carry some cash as backup.
Flagship public hospitals run dedicated foreigner channels. Peking Union Medical College Hospital (北京协和医院), one of China's most respected, has its International Medical Services (国际医疗部) with separate appointment lines for embassy staff and for international visitors, plus an English interface in its official app where you (or a Chinese contact booking on your behalf) can reserve a slot; its emergency department runs 24/7. City super-apps also carry hospital booking in English-friendly flows: International Service Beijing (京通), Shanghai's Suishenban (随申办), and Zhejiang's Zheliban (浙里办). First-time online booking sometimes requires one in-person counter registration before the app will let you reserve, so build in a buffer.
Public hospitals almost never bill your foreign insurer directly — you pay first and claim reimbursement later. The document that makes a claim possible is the fapiao (发票), China's official tax invoice, plus an itemized cost breakdown. Ask for both at the final settlement window before you leave; a payment screenshot or app receipt alone is usually not accepted by overseas insurers. The international/VIP departments of big public hospitals and private international clinics are the main places that may arrange direct billing with global insurers such as Bupa, Cigna, or Allianz.
FAQ
Will a Chinese hospital treat me if I cannot pay?
Public hospitals are legally required to provide emergency care regardless of payment ability. If you are in a life-threatening situation and cannot pay, they will still treat you and bill you later, sometimes through your embassy. For non-emergency care, they will refuse to provide treatment without payment. Private international hospitals are stricter about upfront payment; they generally will not admit you without a credit card on file.
Can I use my regular home insurance in China?
Unless it is international travel insurance or an international health insurance plan, probably not. Most American domestic health insurance (including most Medicare plans) does not cover care received outside the US. Always travel with dedicated travel medical insurance. It is inexpensive (¥150 to ¥500 for a 2-week policy) and covers gaps your regular insurance leaves open.
How do I find the nearest hospital with English-speaking staff?
Ask your hotel front desk, they often keep a list. Alternatively, search Baidu Maps or Amap for 国际医院 (international hospital) or the name of a specific chain like "United Family" or "Parkway." International SOS has an app for members with live assistance. The US Embassy, UK Consulate, and Australian Embassy all publish lists of foreign-friendly medical providers on their country-specific travel websites; save a PDF before your trip.
What should I do if I need dental care?
Private international dental clinics operate in major cities with Western-trained dentists and English service, at 40 to 60 percent of US private dental rates. Public hospital dental departments are cheaper but harder to navigate in English. For emergency tooth pain, a private dental clinic is the fastest path.
What about mental health care in China?
Access is improving but still limited for foreigners. Private international hospitals in Beijing and Shanghai have English-speaking psychiatrists and counselors. For urgent crises, call 120 to be taken to an ER. For ongoing therapy, many expats use international telemedicine services. If you have a chronic mental health condition, talk to your home provider about maintenance before traveling.
Two practical notes that sit outside the hospital itself. Payment at registration is by Alipay or WeChat Pay at most public hospitals, so having a card linked before you are ill is worth the fifteen minutes. And altitude is the commonest reason travellers end up needing help at all — the West Sichuan guide has the symptom thresholds and names the hospital in Kangding with an altitude ward, which is the kind of thing to know before you need it.
One more thing to do while you are well: put the hospital's Chinese name and address in your notes as characters, not pinyin, because a taxi driver at 02:00 will not read pinyin. The same habit applies to your accommodation — the hotel registration guide explains why the front desk has your passport details on file and why that matters if anyone needs to find you.
