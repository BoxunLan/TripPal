Tashkurgan to Kashgar: The Permit Matters More Than the Fare

The fare is not what stops people on this road. The paperwork is. Tashkurgan sits inside a restricted border zone, and a Chinese tourist visa does not get you through the checkpoints on the way back to Kashgar — you need a Xinjiang border permit (边防证) that names Tashkurgan, and the checkpoints scan for it. Travellers without one get turned around. I watched it happen at the second checkpoint in September 2024.
Getting the permit
The permit itself is free. What varies is how long it takes to get one, and that decides how you plan the trip.

The airport desk is the one worth knowing about. Most guides send you to an agency and a week's wait for a document you can collect on landing. The PSB counter works too, but its hours are narrow and it processes against Chinese national ID cards by default — as a foreigner you present passport plus a valid China visa, and you may be sent to the foreigner counter near the airport anyway.
Guesthouses in Tashkurgan can sometimes arrange one after the fact if you arrived without it. That is a recovery plan, not a plan.

What the permit does not cover
This catches people who assume one permit covers the region.
Khunjerab Pass, the actual China–Pakistan border, needs a separate permit applied for locally in Tashkurgan. A Tashkurgan permit alone will not get you to the boundary marker.

Yarkand (Shache), if you are continuing that way, has to be named on the permit too. Ask for both when you apply — adding it later means starting over.

The pass itself is open only between 1 May and 15 October, so a permit for it is useless outside those dates.

Checkpoints
Two or three along the 290 km. The routine is the same each time: the bus parks, the driver hands over the passenger manifest, everyone gets off with their documents, you walk through the building for a facial scan and an ID check, luggage sometimes goes through an X-ray, and you reboard when the last person clears.
Budget 15 to 40 minutes per stop depending on how many buses are ahead of you. Two things make it easier: keep your passport and permit in a jacket pocket rather than the bottom of a backpack, and do not photograph the checkpoint buildings. A digital ID on your phone is not accepted — the original passport, or for Chinese nationals the physical second-generation ID card.
Enforcement is not even. Han Chinese travellers are sometimes waved through on ID alone; Uyghur and Kazakh travellers face longer checks with the same documents.

Take the bus if you are on a budget and can make a 10:00 departure. Take a van if you missed it, or if your luggage does not want to live in an underbus compartment for five hours.
There is a third option locals use. The Liaison Office runs its own vehicles between the two towns for staff errands. They cost a little more than the scheduled bus and the driver will pull over at Karakul Lake and for Muztagh Ata — the scheduled bus will not stop for scenery at all.
Where things actually leave from
In Tashkurgan: the Passenger Transport Station on Xingfu Road, about 1.5 km north of the Stone City ruins. Small, signposted, five to fifteen minutes on foot from most guesthouses. Window hours 08:30 to 17:30. Vans do not use it — they collect from hotel lobbies, from Chenguang Eden on Xiyu Avenue, or from the intersection by the government square.
In Kashgar, going the other direction, the useful address is the Tashkurgan County Liaison Office (塔县办事处) at No. 16 Tiannan Road — not the main long-distance bus station. The official station out in Guangzhou New City sits on the edge of town with few departures, and going there tends to cost you half a day. Shared cars fill and leave from the Liaison Office, and it is where drivers and other travellers arrange carpools.
Season
April to October: two or three departures a day, the main one around 10:00, an afternoon service on busy days. During the late-April apricot blossom window and the July–August holidays, book the day before.
November to March: one morning departure. Snow closes the Ghez canyon or the stretch near Karakul on roughly 5 to 15 days a winter, and when it closes there is no alternative route — you wait. Ask your guesthouse about road status the evening before and keep a spare day in the itinerary.
Tickets
At the station window with your passport, paying cash, Alipay or WeChat Pay. Or in advance through the 新疆客运联网售票 mini programme inside WeChat, one to three days out, which matters in peak season. Foreign passport numbers sometimes fail validation on the first attempt — restart the app and re-enter. Either way you show the physical passport at the gate.
Vans are not bookable online. Ask the front desk the evening before and pay the driver on the day.
The option older guides miss
There is no railway to Tashkurgan and there probably will not be one soon. But Tashkurgan Khunjerab Airport opened on 23 December 2022 and flies to Kashgar — China's closest airport to Pakistan, Tajikistan and Afghanistan. It turns an all-day road journey into a short hop.
Most people still take the road, and they are right to. The Karakoram Highway is the reason to be here: Karakul Lake at 3,600 m, Muztagh Ata at 7,546 m in view for the better part of an hour, then the red rock of the Ghez canyon as you drop toward Kashgar. Sit on the left-hand side going north.
Small things that help
Tashkurgan is at about 3,100 m and Kashgar at 1,300 m. Going down fixes headaches. Going up, take the first night easy.

Carry ¥200 in small notes. Digital payment is unreliable at checkpoints and rural rest stops.

Download offline maps before you leave — signal between the two cities comes and goes. See getting online in China for which app to use.

No drones. Luggage scans find them and you will lose hours at best.

Baisha Lake charges ¥40, but the mirror-reflection view is free from the public platform on the north bank. Be there before 11:00, while the wind is still down.

Planning the wider region: the northern Xinjiang loop covers Urumqi, Turpan and Sayram Lake, where a standard tourist visa is enough and no border permit is involved. Further east, the K497 to Dunhuang is the Silk Road by rail. And if the visa itself is still open, the tourist visa guide covers what you need before any of this.
FAQ
Do I need a border permit if I already have a China visa?
Yes. They are separate documents. A tourist visa does not clear the checkpoints between Kashgar and Tashkurgan; you need a Xinjiang border permit naming Tashkurgan as well. The permit is free — the fastest place to get one is the immigration desk on the second floor of Kashgar Airport, in 15 to 30 minutes.
How much is the bus?
¥119 at the Tashkurgan station window, for the roughly 290 km and 4.5 to 5 hours including checkpoints. A seat in a shared 7-seater is ¥150–200 and gets you door-to-door from your guesthouse.
What happens if I miss the morning bus?
Ask your guesthouse to put you in a shared van; they fill and leave through the morning, so you still reach Kashgar the same day. Do not hire a private taxi on your own — drivers ask ¥500–800 for the route.
Can I break the journey at Karakul Lake?
Not on a single through ticket. Book Tashkurgan to Karakul as its own leg for about ¥50, stay at a yurt camp, then pick up a Kashgar-bound bus or van the next morning. Departures from Karakul are less predictable, so keep a day of slack.
