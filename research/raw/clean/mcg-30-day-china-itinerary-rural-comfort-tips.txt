Thirty Days in Rural China: The Registration Problem Comes First

The thing that derails a rural China trip is not the distance or the altitude. It is a village homestay accepting your booking online and then apologising at the door because it cannot register a foreign passport with the local police.
Chinese law requires every foreign visitor to be registered within 24 hours of arrival. City hotels do it automatically at check-in. A family-run inn in a Dong village may not have the licence, the terminal, or anyone who has done it before. Plan the route around that and the rest of a month in Yunnan, Guangxi and Guizhou is genuinely easy.
The registration problem, and how to not have it
Two separate requirements get confused. Every visitor must be registered with the police within 24 hours. Separately, the accommodation itself needs to be licensed to host foreign guests. A place can fail the second while being perfectly nice.
Book through Trip.com in English and look for listings that say foreigner-friendly or international guests welcome.

Message the property before you pay — Trip.com has a chat — and ask the direct question: can you register a foreign passport?

Keep one known-good hotel in every base town as the fallback. You will probably use it once.

Erhai Lake has an extra layer: a Dali Homestay Management and Promotion Regulation took effect on 1 January 2026, the province's first, and it has tightened what lakeside properties can operate at all. Confirm your Shuanglang booking is still trading before you travel. How hotel check-in works for foreigners covers the paperwork side.

Two logistics notes that are not obvious from a map. Shaxi has no station and no long-distance bus terminal — everything funnels through Jianchuan first. And Longji is a working hillside between 300 and 1,100 m, not a viewpoint you drive to: leave the large suitcase in Guilin and go up with a daypack.

Base in Xingping rather than central Yangshuo for the quietest stretch of the Li River — Yangshuo town is lively and loud, Xingping empties out after the day boats leave ( the Guilin and Yangshuo guide ).
At Longji, what you see depends entirely on the farming calendar, and Ping'an and Dazhai flood on different dates. Ping'an has more restaurants and easier access; Dazhai is the harder walk and the better photograph.
Pacing this for a body in its fifties or sixties
Two bases a week is the ceiling. The failure mode on a month-long trip is not injury, it is the accumulated fatigue of moving every second day, and it usually shows up as illness in week three.
One rest day every five or six. The Guilin and Kunming stops in the table above are exactly that.

First class on any rail leg over two hours. It is ¥100–200 more and buys a wider seat and a quieter carriage.

One collapsible trekking pole. Dazhai, upper Zhaoxing and Xingping's Laozhai hill are all stone steps.

Bottled or boiled water only. Rural tap water is not treated to a standard worth trusting.

Altitude is not the issue on this route. Dali is near 2,000 m and everything in Guangxi and Guizhou is far lower — this is deliberately a mid-elevation itinerary.
Visa, money, internet
Thirty days rules out visa-free transit entirely: the 240-hour policy caps you at ten days and needs an onward ticket to a third country. This trip needs a proper L visa, or one of the 30-day visa-free arrangements if your passport has one.
Set up Alipay and WeChat Pay before you fly. Cash is rarely useful in cities and is sometimes refused in rural shops, which surprises people who expect the opposite.
For connectivity you have a real choice on this route. A roaming eSIM gives you Google and WhatsApp throughout. Or go native — Amap and Trip.com cover every stop on this itinerary well, and the Chinese-app route works better here than it does in a city.

FAQ
Will rural homestays accept a foreign passport?
Not all of them. Every visitor must be registered with local police within 24 hours, and a small family-run inn may not be licensed to do it. Book through Trip.com listings that say foreigner-friendly, ask the property directly before paying, and keep a known-good hotel as backup in each base town.
Is a 30-day southwestern route suitable in your fifties or sixties?
Yes, at two bases a week with a rest day every five or six. Altitude tops out around 2,000 m at Dali and the Guangxi and Guizhou stops are much lower, so this route avoids the altitude problem entirely.
Can I do this on visa-free transit?
No. The 240-hour transit caps the stay at ten days and requires an onward ticket to a third country. A month needs an L visa or a 30-day visa-free arrangement if your passport qualifies.
What does the trip cost?
Roughly US$2,900 to US$3,800 per person for thirty days of land costs at mid-range homestays, plus US$900–1,500 for the international flight.
