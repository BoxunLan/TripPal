No. Netflix does not work in mainland China, and the reason is double: Netflix has never launched a Chinese service because it could not get a streaming license, and the Great Firewall blocks netflix.com and the app's servers anyway. Netflix's own help center lists China alongside North Korea and Syria as places where the service is unavailable. A VPN can restore access, but only one that beats both the Firewall and Netflix's VPN detection, which blocks known VPN server IPs with a proxy error. Your two most reliable options in 2026: download shows to your phone before you fly (ad-free plans allow up to 100 active downloads per device), or use a travel eSIM, whose roaming data bypasses the Firewall without a VPN. Do not count on the hotel TV - Netflix killed phone-to-TV casting for most devices in late 2025.
Honestly, this is one of the first questions my clients ask once flights are booked - usually right after "does my phone work there?" The answer disappoints people, but the fix is simple if you prepare before takeoff. Here's the full picture.
Why Netflix has no service in China
Most blocked apps in China share one story: the Great Firewall cut them off. Netflix is different, because it fails twice over.
First, there is no Chinese Netflix. China requires foreign streaming services to hold local licenses and route content through approved partners, with every title subject to content review. Netflix explored a licensing deal years ago, briefly sold a few originals to the Chinese platform iQiyi, and then gave up on the market. Today, Netflix's own help center is blunt about it: the service is not available in China, Crimea, North Korea, Russia, or Syria. There is no Chinese payment option, no Chinese app store listing, no local content library. Nothing to sign up for.
Second, the site is blocked anyway. Even though Netflix never launched, the Great Firewall blocks netflix.com and the CDN endpoints the app talks to. So a foreigner arriving with an active US, UK, or Australian subscription hits a wall the moment the phone connects to Chinese Wi-Fi or a local SIM: the app opens, the thumbnails may load from cache, and then everything spins and times out.
This double lock matters for your expectations. YouTube, Instagram, and WhatsApp are blocked but otherwise fully functional services - fix the network path and they work perfectly. Netflix adds its own second gate on top, which brings us to VPNs.

Can you watch Netflix in China with a VPN?
Yes, but this is the trickiest VPN use case in China, because you need to clear two separate hurdles at once.
Hurdle one: the Great Firewall. Ordinary VPN protocols get detected and throttled or dropped inside China. You need a provider with obfuscated servers - connections disguised as regular HTTPS traffic - that still works on the mainland. Only a handful of providers maintain this reliably; our tested shortlist is in the best VPN for China guide .
Hurdle two: Netflix's own VPN detection. Netflix maintains a database of known VPN and data-center IP addresses. Connect through a flagged IP and you either see the "you seem to be using a VPN or proxy" error or get quietly limited to a small generic library. This detection got noticeably stricter through 2025 and 2026, and smaller VPN providers lose the cat-and-mouse game constantly.
The practical result: a VPN that sails through the Firewall for Gmail and Instagram may still fail for Netflix, because its streaming-friendly IPs are burned. What works in 2026 is a major provider that both operates obfuscated servers for China and actively rotates fresh IPs for streaming. Even then, expect to switch servers a few times before one plays, and expect 1080p rather than 4K - your traffic is making a long encrypted detour before it ever reaches Netflix.
One more nuance people miss: the library you see follows your VPN exit country. Exit through Japan and you get Japanese Netflix, minus anything the detection blocks. If a specific show is the whole point, check it plays through your setup at home before you fly - do not debug this from a hotel room in Chengdu.
The smarter play: download everything before you fly
Here's the thing nobody tells you: for a one- or two-week trip, you don't need Netflix to work in China. You need 20 hours of content on your phone, and Netflix's offline downloads handle that with zero workarounds - downloaded titles play on a plane, on the Beijing subway, anywhere, with no connection at all.
The rules, straight from Netflix's help center and current as of July 2026:
Ad-free plans (Standard at $19.99/month, Premium at $26.99/month) allow up to 100 active downloads per device . Standard covers 2 download devices, Premium covers 6.

The ad-supported plan ($8.99/month) is limited to 15 downloads per device per calendar month - workable for a short trip, tight for a long one.

Unwatched downloads expire , typically after 7 days, though some titles last longer and a few expire faster depending on the studio deal.

Once you press play , some titles start a 48-hour clock before they expire, even offline.

A small number of titles have a yearly re-download cap, and some (mostly licensed movies) can't be downloaded at all.

Those expiry rules shape the strategy. Download the night before your flight, not a week early. Favor Netflix originals - they have the most generous download terms. And if you're traveling two weeks or more, note that renewing an expired download requires a connection back to Netflix, which puts you back in VPN territory mid-trip. A German client of mine learned this on day 9 of a Yangtze cruise: half her queue expired mid-river, and there was nothing to be done about it until Shanghai. Front-load more than you think you need.
Downloads live in the app on the device that downloaded them - you can't move them to another phone or push them to a TV. Grab them over home Wi-Fi; a season in HD runs several gigabytes.
The same download-before-you-fly logic applies to maps, translation, and guides, by the way - it's why the MyChinaGuide app keeps its city guides and survival tutorials fully offline.
Hotel TVs, casting, and why your usual tricks fail
Every trick you use in hotels elsewhere fails in China, and one of them now fails everywhere.
No Netflix app on the TV. Chinese hotel TVs run domestic systems loaded with local platforms like iQiyi and Tencent Video. There is no Netflix app to sign in to, and no Chinese app store carries one.

Casting from your phone is effectively dead. In late 2025, Netflix removed support for casting from the mobile app to most TVs and streaming devices - only ancient remote-less Chromecasts still accept it, and the ad-supported plan can't cast at all. Even before that change, Chromecast hardware needed Google services to function, and Google is blocked in China.

HDMI from a laptop is your one realistic path to a big screen: hotel TVs usually do have an HDMI port. But your laptop still needs a working VPN to stream, so you've just moved the hard problem, not solved it.

My honest advice after years of escorting clients through this: treat the hotel TV as furniture. Your phone or tablet, loaded with downloads, is your in-China cinema.
The eSIM roaming exception
There's one clean way to reach Netflix in China with no VPN at all: roaming data. When you use a travel eSIM (or your home carrier's international roaming), your data traffic tunnels back through a gateway outside mainland China before it touches the open internet. The Great Firewall never filters it, and Netflix sees a normal IP from the gateway country - so the app just works, showing that country's library.
Three caveats before you get excited:
This only covers cellular data. Join hotel Wi-Fi and you're behind the Firewall again. Keep Wi-Fi off for the apps that matter.

Streaming eats data. Netflix burns roughly 1 GB per hour in standard definition and up to 3 GB per hour in HD. On a typical 20 GB travel eSIM costing around ¥180 / $25, a nightly binge will bankrupt your allowance in under a week. Fine for one episode in an airport queue; wrong tool for a series.

Speeds vary by provider and city. Most travel eSIMs handle SD streaming fine; HD can stutter at peak evening hours.

For blocked-app access in general, an eSIM plus offline downloads covers most travelers without a VPN. See the best eSIM for China roundup for current plans and prices, and the full blocked apps list for what else you'll need a workaround for.
What locals watch instead
Out of curiosity more than necessity: China's streaming market runs on iQiyi, Tencent Video, Youku, and Bilibili, plus Mango TV for variety shows. A few international titles appear there in licensed, censored versions, but catalogs are overwhelmingly Chinese-language with limited English subtitles, and signing up generally requires a Chinese phone number. As a visitor, don't plan around them - though flipping through what's on the hotel TV is genuinely fun cultural browsing, and hotel-lobby conversations about whatever drama is currently huge are a great icebreaker.
Will my Netflix downloads play in China without a VPN?
Yes. Downloaded titles play entirely offline, so the Great Firewall never enters the picture. Just mind the expiry rules: unwatched downloads typically last 7 days, and some titles expire 48 hours after you first press play. Download fresh content the night before your flight and favor Netflix originals for the longest shelf life.
Can I just sign up for a Chinese version of Netflix?
No such thing exists. Netflix has never operated in mainland China and its help center explicitly lists China as unavailable. Locals use domestic platforms like iQiyi and Tencent Video, which need a Chinese phone number and carry mostly Chinese-language catalogs.
Does Netflix work in Hong Kong and Macau?
Yes, fully and officially. The Great Firewall applies to mainland China only. Hong Kong and Macau have their own Netflix libraries, and your subscription works there with no VPN - a nice reset stop if your itinerary passes through.
Why does my VPN work for everything except Netflix?
Because Netflix runs its own VPN detection on top of the Firewall problem. It blocks known VPN server IPs, so a connection that unblocks Gmail can still trigger Netflix's proxy error. Switch to a different server, ideally one your provider labels for streaming, and pick a provider known to keep beating detection.
