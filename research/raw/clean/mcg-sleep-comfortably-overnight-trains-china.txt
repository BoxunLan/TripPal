You booked a hard sleeper from Beijing to Xi'an because the ticket cost ¥320 and a flight was ¥1,100. Now you're staring at a six-berth open cabin wondering whether you'll get any sleep at all. The honest answer: yes, if you choose the right class, the right berth, and bring four small items most first-timers forget.
Overnight trains in China are not the chaotic experience travel blogs from 2010 describe. The rolling stock on Z and T routes is clean, climate-controlled, and boarded by families, business travelers, and students. The trick is understanding what you actually booked, where you should sleep inside the carriage, and how to handle the universal challenges of shared bathrooms, noise, and food.

Understanding China's overnight train classes
Chinese Railways runs four long-distance train categories that include sleeper cars. The letter prefix on your ticket tells you everything about speed, comfort, and price.
Z trains (直达, zhídá) are non-stop express sleepers. They skip small stations, run mostly overnight, and are the most comfortable legacy long-haul option. Beijing-Shanghai Z1/Z2 is the flagship.
T trains (特快, tèkuài) are "fast" trains with limited stops. Slightly older rolling stock than Z, similar sleeper layout, a bit cheaper.
K trains (快速, kuàisù) are slow trains that stop often. Oldest carriages, cheapest fares, lots of hard seat passengers. Fine for short overnight hops but not your first choice for a 14-hour ride.
D night trains (动卧, dòngwò) are overnight high-speed trains with fully enclosed sleeper compartments. D313 Beijing-Shanghai departs around 19:30 and arrives before 08:00, running at HSR speeds. These are the most comfortable overnight option in China.
Hard sleeper: the backpacker standard
Hard sleeper (硬卧, yìngwò) is a six-berth open cabin with no door. Three bunks stack on each side of a narrow aisle. You get a thin mattress, a pillow, a clean sheet, and a quilt handed to you at boarding. The aisle has fold-down stools and a small window table where passengers drink tea and eat instant noodles.
Privacy is minimal, but the cabin is quieter than Western travelers expect. Lights go out around 22:00, conductors enforce silence, and most Chinese passengers are polite about phone volume. 2026 prices for hard sleeper run ¥280 to ¥700 depending on distance and berth position.
Soft sleeper: the comfortable sweet spot
Soft sleeper (软卧, ruǎnwò) is a four-berth enclosed compartment with a sliding door that locks from inside. Two bunks on each side, proper mattresses, a shared window table, a thermos of hot water, and power outlets. You can close the door and actually relax.
This is where most foreign travelers should spend their money. The difference between hard sleeper and soft sleeper on a 14-hour ride is the difference between surviving and sleeping. 2026 prices run ¥450 to ¥1,100.
Deluxe soft sleeper: the splurge
Deluxe soft sleeper (高级软卧, gāojí ruǎnwò) exists on a handful of Z trains including Beijing-Shanghai. Two berths per compartment, private sink, sometimes a door to an adjoining cabin, and genuinely hotel-grade bedding. Prices start around ¥900 and climb past ¥1,500 on premium routes.
Hard seat: only if you must
Hard seat (硬座, yìngzuò) is a padded upright bench and it is not a sleeping option for an overnight journey unless your budget is brutal. ¥90 to ¥200 for rides where you will not sleep. Skip it.

Choosing the right berth position
Every sleeper car assigns berth numbers, and the number you pick changes your night more than the train class does.
Hard sleeper berth ranking
Middle bunk (中铺, zhōngpù) is the best. You sit up without hitting the ceiling, you skip the daytime foot traffic that plagues the lower bunk, and you do not climb as high as the top.

Lower bunk (下铺, xiàpù) is the most expensive and turns into shared seating during the day as other passengers sit on the edge while eating. You lose privacy from 06:00 to 22:00. Good only if you have knee problems.

Upper bunk (上铺, shàngpù) is the cheapest. You cannot sit up, ventilation is warmer near the ceiling, and the climb is awkward with a full bladder at 03:00.

Soft sleeper berth ranking
Only two levels exist. Lower berths are slightly more expensive but give you headroom, easier bathroom access, and a place to sit during the day. Upper berths save you ¥50-80 and come with a small pull-down ladder. Both are comfortable.
Security: practical habits, not paranoia
China's long-distance trains are statistically very safe. Conductors walk the aisles, cabins are well-lit, and most reported incidents involve unattended phones left on cabin tables. Use common sense.
Chain your backpack to the bunk frame with a small padlock and a wire cable. A ¥45 retractable cable from any Taobao kiosk works.

Sleep with your passport, cash, and phone in a zipped waist pouch or a pillowcase tied to your wrist.

In hard sleeper, place your main bag on the overhead rack above your own bunk, never above someone else's.

Soft sleeper: lock the compartment door from inside after lights-out. The latch is on the wall opposite the door.

Do not leave valuables on the window table when you walk to the bathroom. Take your phone.

Bathroom and washroom strategy
Every sleeper car has two toilets at the ends and a washing alcove with metal basins and cold water taps. A few realities to plan for.
Hot water comes from a thermos station at one end of the car, refilled every few hours by an attendant. Use it for instant noodles, tea, and diluting bottled water if you dislike cold drinks.

Squat toilets are standard on older T and K trains. Z and D trains usually have one Western sit-down toilet per car. Check both ends before committing.

Peak bathroom hours are 06:30 to 08:00 when everyone prepares to disembark. Brush your teeth at 22:30 the night before or 05:30 early.

Toilet paper is not reliably stocked. Bring a small pack.

Bathrooms are locked at every station stop to prevent waste discharge on platforms. If you see the red light, wait until the train leaves the platform.

Food, hot water, and snacks
Train attendants push a cart through the cabin three times a day selling box meals (盒饭, héfàn) at ¥35 to ¥60. Quality is acceptable rice-plus-vegetables-plus-meat. Not memorable, not bad.
Every Chinese station has a convenience shop on the platform selling instant noodles, vacuum-packed chicken feet, boiled eggs, fruit, and bottled water. Longer stops of 10+ minutes let you step off and restock. The conductor announces how long you can stay off, and the whistle before boarding is loud enough to hear.
The civilized move is to board with your own kit: a cup of Master Kong instant noodles (¥5), a bottle of water, an orange, and a small bag of sunflower seeds. Hot water is free from the cabin thermos. You will eat like a local for ¥15.
What to pack for a comfortable night
Earplugs (foam, not silicone). Snorers exist in every cabin and the train horn sounds on bridges.

Sleep mask. Cabin lights dim but reading lights from neighboring bunks do not.

Travel pillow or small inflatable pillow. The standard-issue pillow is thin.

Light scarf or sarong. Doubles as extra blanket in winter and privacy curtain for your hard sleeper berth.

Flip-flops or slip-on sandals. For bathroom trips. Do not walk barefoot.

Small microfiber towel and travel-size toiletries in a zip bag.

Phone, portable battery (20,000mAh or smaller), charging cable. Every carriage has outlets but not always next to your berth.

Snacks and a 1L water bottle.

Toilet paper, wet wipes, hand sanitizer.

A zipped waist pouch for passport, cash, and phone while sleeping.

What NOT to bring
Giant hard-shell suitcases. The overhead rack in hard sleeper is narrow. Use a soft duffel or backpack under 65L.

Valuables you cannot replace. Leave jewelry and the second laptop in your hotel locker.

Strong-smelling food. Durian is banned, and your cabinmates will not appreciate stinky tofu at midnight.

Alcohol in visible bottles. Quiet drinking is tolerated but public drunkenness gets a conductor warning.

Drones with uncharged batteries. Lithium rules are enforced at the station X-ray. Under 160Wh, in carry-on.

FAQ
Are overnight trains in China safe for solo female travelers?
Yes. Soft sleeper compartments lock from inside and are usually shared with families or couples. Hard sleeper is public but well-lit and patrolled by conductors. Tens of thousands of solo female backpackers ride Chinese sleepers every week without incident. Take standard precautions and sleep with your phone and passport in a waist pouch.
Can I book overnight trains without a Chinese phone number?
Yes. The 12306 English app accepts passport registration and foreign payment cards (UnionPay, Alipay Tour Pass, or a credit card linked through Alipay). Trip.com International also books the same inventory in English and accepts Visa and Mastercard. See our separate guide to booking China train tickets without WeChat for step-by-step screenshots.
How far in advance should I book an overnight sleeper?
Tickets go on sale 15 days before departure on 12306. For Z trains on Beijing-Shanghai, Beijing-Xi'an, and Beijing-Guangzhou during Chinese public holidays (Spring Festival, May Day, National Day), book the moment sales open at 06:00 Beijing time. For off-peak travel, 3-5 days ahead is usually safe.
What temperature are sleeper cars in winter?
Heated to 18-22°C (64-72°F). You will not be cold. In summer, air conditioning keeps cars at 22-24°C. Pack a light layer for both seasons, not a heavy jacket.
Can I upgrade my hard sleeper to soft sleeper after boarding?
Sometimes. Walk to the conductor's office (often marked 列车长办公席) in one of the middle cars with your ticket and passport. If soft sleeper berths are unsold, you can pay the difference in cash or via Alipay QR. No guarantee, and the conductor will turn you down during peak season.
How do I handle luggage on an overnight sleeper?
Hard sleeper carriages have an overhead rack that runs above each set of six berths, roughly the size of a large carry-on per person. Soft sleeper compartments have both overhead space and an under-bunk nook for one medium suitcase per passenger. Very large hard-shell suitcases do fit but you will frustrate your cabinmates by blocking the aisle while loading them. A 65L soft duffel is the ideal Chinese sleeper bag — compressible, throwable onto the top rack, and easy to fish small items out of at 06:00.
What language do conductors speak on overnight trains?
Mandarin. Most conductors on Beijing-Shanghai and Beijing-Guangzhou Z trains know a handful of English words (ticket, passport, bathroom, dining car) but do not expect fluent conversation. A translation app on your phone handles anything complicated. Show your paper or electronic ticket when asked and the conductor will lead you to your berth without needing shared language.
