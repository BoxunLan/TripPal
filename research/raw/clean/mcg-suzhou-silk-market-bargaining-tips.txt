Suzhou Silk: What It Costs, and What the Fake-Silk Warnings Get Wrong

Two things can go wrong when you buy silk in Suzhou, and they are not equally likely.
The one everyone warns you about is fake silk. It is real, but it is mostly confined to tour-bus showrooms and scenic-area stalls. When the Suzhou Market Supervision Bureau sampled 50 batches of silk quilts in 2022 — pulled from factories, storefronts and e-commerce — 49 passed. The single failure was a pH reading from over-aggressive washing, not polyester fill.
The one that actually costs you money is paying the first price. Asking prices in the markets run two to three times what the same item sells for once you negotiate. That gap is the whole subject.

Below the fair-price column you are not getting a bargain, you are getting a blend. Mulberry silk has a material cost floor. A ¥50 "silk" scarf is polyester and there is no version of that sentence where it isn't.

How the number comes down
The arithmetic is consistent enough to plan around. Counter at 30 to 35 percent of the first ask. If they open at ¥800, you say ¥250. They will look wounded. The price lands somewhere between 40 and 60 percent of where it started — ¥350 to ¥450 on that ¥800 — and both of you knew it would.
You are not taking food off anyone's table. Stall margins over wholesale run 200 to 300 percent, so your number still clears.
Walking away works exactly once. Say 太贵了 (tài guì le), shake your head, move toward the next stall. If there is room left, you get called back before you reach it. If you come back on your own five minutes later, that lever is spent and you will pay close to their last quote.
One calibration that matters: Suzhou is not a hard-bargaining city. At the old-brand storefronts on Guanqian Street, grinding someone down is read as a loss of face on both sides — expect 10 to 20 percent, not half. The 30-percent opener belongs at the tourist stalls, not in a shop that has been there since the 1920s.
The 60-second check
Do this before you negotiate. You cannot bargain your way out of polyester.
Pull a thread from an inside seam and burn it. Vendors selling real silk hand you the lighter; the ones who refuse have told you what you needed to know.

Two hand tests while you are holding it. Real silk is cool on first contact and warms fast — hold it between your palms for ten seconds and it is noticeably warm when you open them. Polyester stays at room temperature and feels slightly slippery. And pinch a fold, rub it against itself: silk makes a faint squeak, the sound the trade calls scroop. Polyester is silent.
What the label has to say
The word "silk" on its own means nothing here. 丝 (sī) is used in marketing for anything that feels silky. The words you want are 桑蚕丝 (sāngcánsī, mulberry silk) or 柞蚕丝 (zhàcánsī, tussah silk), on a woven-in label rather than a paper sticker that lifts with a fingernail.
A legitimate quilt is labelled to the GB/T 24252 national standard, and that tag has to state three things: fibre type, fill weight, and grade. A tag missing any of them is not a tag.
Quilts are where the fraud actually lives
Everything above is checkable by touching the fabric. A quilt hides its filling, which is why the fakes concentrate here — a silk shell over a token handful of silk fibre sewn near the opening, polyester behind it.
Real Suzhou quilts have a zippered or buttoned corner precisely so you can look inside. A vendor who won't open it is answering your question.

Real fill is long, fluffy, and holds a faint lustre even loose. Polyester fill is shorter and too uniform.

Burn a fibre from the filling, not the shell.

Weigh it. A quilt with 1.5 kg of silk fill should come to 2 to 2.5 kg with the shell. If it feels featherlight, the fill isn't silk.

Rub the opened fill and hold it near your arm. Silk doesn't hold a static charge; synthetic does, and your arm hair will tell you.

Ask for the certificate. Real quilts ship with one stating grade and fill weight.

Where to buy, and where the risk really is
There is no single Suzhou silk market. There are three places, and the difference between them is the difference between the two risks at the top of this page.
Suzhou No.1 Silk Factory store and the stalls around it, near the railway station. The factory store is government-certified and has run since the 1920s. Prices there are fixed and about 10 to 20 percent above the stalls — which makes it the most useful price reference in the city. Check the factory number, then bargain outside with it in your head.

Zhen Hu (镇湖), 40 minutes west, for Su-style embroidery. This is a working embroidery town, not a market. Prices reflect labour hours.

The old town shops around Pingjiang Road and Guanqian Street. Small boutiques, storefront prices, light bargaining only.

The tour-bus "silk factory" is a fourth category and belongs in a different sentence. Go for the silkworm and reeling demonstration — it is genuinely worth seeing — and treat the showroom afterwards as a museum gift shop. That is where the 98-percent pass rate stops applying.
If you were sold a fake
Under China's Consumer Rights Protection Law, proven counterfeit goods entitle you to 退一赔三 (tuì yī péi sān) — refund one, compensate three. A full refund plus three times the purchase price. Keep the receipt and the hangtag; without them you have a story rather than a claim.
This is worth knowing before you buy, because it changes how a vendor behaves when you mention it.
Paying and getting it home
Most stalls take WeChat Pay and Alipay . Cash still buys you something: on purchases over ¥1,000, asking for a cash price often takes another 5 percent off, because it saves the vendor the processing fee. Carry ¥1,500 to ¥2,000 if you are quilt-shopping.
Card acceptance is patchy — the factory store and the bigger shops near the station take foreign cards, the Zhen Hu stalls generally do not ( where foreign cards work in China ).
There is no tourist VAT refund on silk, so the price you agree is the price. Airport duty free sells silk at two to three times the market. For quilts, most shops ship internationally via DHL or SF Express for ¥200 to ¥400, 7 to 14 days, with your own country's import duty on top.
Suzhou is 25 minutes from Shanghai by high-speed rail, so this is an easy day trip with a bulky purchase at the end of it — the train guide covers luggage on the return leg.
FAQ
How much should I offer?
Thirty to thirty-five percent of the first asking price at a market stall. Expect to settle at 40 to 60 percent of where they started. At established storefronts, 10 to 20 percent off is the realistic range and pushing harder reads as rude.
How do I know the silk is real?
Burn a thread from an inside seam. Mulberry silk smells like burning hair and leaves ash that crumbles; polyester smells like plastic and melts into a hard bead. Then check the label says 桑蚕丝, woven in, not a paper sticker.
Is fake silk actually common in Suzhou?
Less than the warnings suggest, in formal channels. A 2022 Suzhou Market Supervision Bureau sampling of 50 quilt batches from factories, shops and e-commerce found 49 compliant. The risk concentrates in tour-bus showrooms and scenic-area stalls, not in the certified factory store or the old-brand shops.
