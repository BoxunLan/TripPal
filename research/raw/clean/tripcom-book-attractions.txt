How to Book Chinese Attractions Without a Chinese Phone Number
This is the single biggest worry for foreign visitors, and the answer is that you don't need a Chinese number for most of the famous sights. Here are the routes that work.

Option 1: Trip.com
The simplest route. You register with an email address and a foreign phone number, pay with an international card, and the booking flow asks for passport details directly. Refunds and changes are handled in English, which matters more than people expect when plans shift. The trade-off is a small booking fee on some products.
Option 2: Official English Websites
Some venues run a foreign-visitor channel with email registration and passport entry, and the Palace Museum is the best-known example. Coverage is patchy rather than universal, and payment with a foreign card can fail on some official sites, so keep this as a price check rather than your only plan.

Option 3: WeChat Mini-Programs
For venues that only sell through WeChat, install WeChat before you travel and set up WeChat Pay with an international card. Some mini-programs accept foreign phone numbers for the verification code, and a few major museums specifically added foreign-number support. Others still expect a mainland number, which is why this route is a fallback rather than a default.

Option 4: Ask a Human
Hotel concierges in Beijing, Shanghai and Xi'an book attraction tickets for guests routinely, and they can use their own verified accounts.
A licensed local guide can reserve on your behalf, which is often the only realistic way into the Palace Museum or National Museum during peak dates.
If you have a friend in mainland China, they can book for you, but your passport details still have to be entered exactly, and you still carry the original passport at the gate.

How to Book China Attraction Tickets with a Passport for Foreigners
This is the part that actually matters, and it's simpler than the horror stories suggest. You book online with your passport details, pay with an international card, and enter with the original passport in your hand. The whole system is built on real-name verification (实名制), which means the name on the booking and the document you present at the gate must be the same person, spelled the same way. Follow the seven steps below in order and you'll get through it the first time; skip the name-format step and you'll be explaining yourself at a turnstile.
Step 1: Pick the Attraction and Find Its Release Window
Booking windows vary widely and they're the reason people miss out. The Palace Museum releases tickets seven days ahead at 20:00 Beijing time, Mogao Caves open up to 30 days ahead, Jiuzhaigou can be booked anywhere from about 14 to 90 days out depending on the platform, and the National Museum of China fills roughly a week ahead. Set an alarm rather than checking when you remember.

Step 2: Create Your Account Before Tickets Go Live
Do this the day before, not at the moment of release. Register with an email address and your foreign phone number, and for anything requiring WeChat, install WeChat and set up WeChat Pay with an international card while you still have a comfortable connection. Accounts created in a panic, mid-release, are how people end up with no ticket.

Step 3: Enter Your Passport Details Exactly as Printed
This is the step that decides whether you get in. Copy your details from the machine-readable zone at the bottom of your passport photo page, not from the decorative printing at the top, because the machine-readable version is what the gates and the booking systems expect.

Step 4: Choose the Date, Time Slot and Ticket Type
Many venues use timed entry, and the Palace Museum splits the day into a morning session and an afternoon session with different entry cut-offs, so pick deliberately. For a first visit, morning slots are almost always the right call: cooler, quieter and more forgiving if you're running late from a hotel on the other side of town.
Step 5: Pay with an International Card
Trip.com accepts international cards directly, and the booking flow is in English with support to match. Official English websites sometimes fail with foreign cards, and WeChat-based channels need WeChat Pay configured in advance. Whatever you use, screenshot the order confirmation, because payment failures during a ticket release are common and you'll want proof if you have to chase it.
Step 6: Save Your Confirmation, QR Code and Order Number
Download or screenshot the ticket, the QR code and the order number, and keep them offline as well as in your email.
Note whether the venue requires you to collect or verify a physical pass first. Mogao Caves, for example, starts your visit at the digital exhibition centre before the caves themselves.
Don't convert an e-ticket into a paper ticket unless you have to, because that usually closes the online refund route.
Step 7: Enter with the Original Passport at the Manual Lane
Arrive 30 to 45 minutes before your slot with the original passport, not a copy, a photo or a PDF.
Head for the staffed verification lane rather than the facial recognition gates, which are built for Chinese ID cards and generally won't process a foreign passport.
Expect a simple document check and a security scan. Keep the passport accessible rather than buried at the bottom of a bag.
Rules That Apply at Almost Every Chinese Attraction
One passport, one ticket per visit date at the strictest venues, so book separately for every traveller.
Children need their own passport and their own reservation, even when entry is free for their age.
Tickets are non-transferable. Booking under someone else's name means refusal at the gate and no guaranteed refund.
The passport you booked with is the passport you must carry. Switching documents voids the booking.
Names, numbers and dates must match everywhere: booking, QR code and passport.

Can You Book or Enter China Attraction Without a Passport? Face Recognition, Photos and PDFs

This is the question I get asked most, and the short answer is that booking requires an accepted ID and entry requires the original document. Chinese platforms and gates are built around real-name verification, and digital copies don't satisfy it.
Facial Recognition (刷脸)
The face-scan lanes at Chinese attractions compare against the national ID database, which foreign passports are not part of. Foreign visitors generally cannot use them.
Some venues capture your photo at a manual counter when you present a passport, which can allow faster entry on subsequent visits to the same venue, such as annual passes at some parks and attractions.
You should always assume you'll use a staffed lane with your original passport, then be pleasantly surprised if anything is faster.
Passport Photos, Scans and PDFs
For entry: generally not accepted. Real-name attractions verify the physical document, and a phone photo or photocopy can't prove validity.
Rare exception: a venue that only scans a QR code and does a visual name check may wave you through on a staff member's discretion, but you should never build a trip around it.
For booking: you'll often type the passport details manually rather than uploading anything, so a clear photo of the photo page helps you copy the machine-readable zone accurately.
Hotel check-in has the same rule, so keep the original passport with you throughout your trip.

If You've Lost Your Passport in China
Report the loss to the local police, get a police report and contact your embassy or consulate for a replacement travel document or emergency passport. Until you have that physical document, real-name attractions will not admit you, and no amount of explaining at the gate will change it. This is one of the strongest arguments for keeping your passport locked up between visits and never leaving it in a hotel safe you don't trust.

The Pattern Behind All of This
Free, famous and capacity-limited equals mandatory reservation. That covers national museums, memorial halls and the square.

Two Rules That Save You Money and Heartache
Never book under someone else's name. Tickets are non-transferable, and a mismatch is grounds for refusal at the gate with no refund guarantee.
Avoid unofficial resellers and "guaranteed ticket" touts. Real-name tickets can't legitimately be resold, so what you're buying is either a tour bundle or a scam.

The Step-by-Step Entry Process
Open the machine-readable zone at the bottom of your passport photo page and read the surname, given names and passport number from it.
Type the surname into the family name field and every given name, including middle names, into the given name field.
Remove accents, umlauts and non-English characters: MÜLLER becomes MULLER, Ø becomes O, Ñ becomes N.
Enter the passport number with no spaces or punctuation.
Check the name order twice. Chinese forms label the boxes by surname and given name, so read the labels rather than copying the order you're used to.
Save the traveller profile before the ticket release time, so you only need to select it at the moment tickets go on sale.

How to Actually Cancel
Go to your order page on the platform you booked with, choose the ticket order, and select refund or cancel.
Pick a reason, submit, and expect the money back within one to three business days to the original payment method.
If you reserved a free ticket and can't attend, cancel it anyway. Releasing it back to the pool is what keeps your account in good standing.
Don't exchange an e-ticket for a paper ticket if there's any chance you'll cancel, because the online refund path usually closes once you do.

Why the No-Show Rule Matters
Because the reservation is free, the platform can't fine you, so it restricts you instead. Two or three unused reservations in a month or a year can get your account suspended from booking for 30 to 90 days. If your plans change, cancel. It takes thirty seconds and it protects the account you may need next week.
Booking at the Gate vs Online: What Still Works
Walk-up ticket offices have not vanished entirely, but they're the exception at famous sights. Where they survive, they usually serve non-real-name attractions, and even then, self-service machines often expect a Chinese ID card. If you must buy on site, use a staffed window with your passport rather than a machine. For anything on the list in the first section of this guide, assume the answer is online only.
Your China Attraction Booking Checklist
Before every Chinese attraction booking, I run through this list. It takes two minutes and it has saved several trips.
Confirm the attraction requires a reservation, and find the release time and window.
Set an alarm for the release, and keep your traveller profile pre-filled.
Copy the name and passport number from the machine-readable zone, with no accents, spaces or dashes.
Book under the exact passport you'll carry, for every person including children.
Save the QR code, order number and confirmation email offline.
Note the cancellation deadline, and cancel if plans change, especially for free tickets.
Carry the original passport on the day, arrive 30 to 45 minutes early, and use the staffed lane.
Last honest note, and it matters most in this topic: Chinese ticketing policies change frequently, and individual venues adjust windows, channels and quotas without much notice. Verify the current rules on the official channel or your booking platform a few days before you travel, and keep your passport details consistent everywhere. Do that, and the real-name system becomes a minor administrative chore instead of the reason you missed the Forbidden City.

Frequently Asked Questions about China attraction tickets

What should I do if China attraction tickets are sold out?

Check the official release time, other sessions, other dates and authorised channels. Use on-site sales only when officially confirmed.

Can I book Chinese attraction tickets with a foreign passport?

Yes, at almost every major attraction, and the passport is usually one of the accepted document types in the booking form. You'll enter your name and passport number, then present the original passport at the gate.

Can I book China attraction without a Chinese phone number?

Yes. Trip.com accepts foreign phone numbers and international cards, and some official channels, including the National Museum of China and Shanghai Museum East, also support foreign numbers. WeChat mini-programs vary: some accept foreign numbers, others still expect a mainland one.

Can someone else book China attraction tickets for me?

Yes, a friend, hotel concierge or guide can complete the booking, but the ticket must be in your name with your own passport details, and you must carry that passport at the gate. Tickets can't be transferred after purchase.

Do I need to print my China attraction ticket?

Most venues accept a QR code or order number on your phone, but a few require collection or verification at a counter before entry, so read your confirmation carefully. A screenshot is worth taking in case the network fails at the gate.

Can I use a photo of my passport at the entrance of China attraction?

Generally no. Real-name venues check the physical document, and a phone photo or photocopy is not accepted. Treat any exception as luck rather than a plan.

What if my name has accents or special characters when booking China attraction?

Convert them to plain English letters. MÜLLER becomes MULLER, and the same applies to Ø, Ñ, Å and similar characters. These systems store Latin letters reliably and mangle everything else.

Can I change the name on a China attraction ticket after booking?

No. Real-name tickets are not transferable. If you booked under the wrong name, cancel and rebook, and check the cancellation terms before the deadline passes.
