Beijing to Chengdu by Train: Complete Guide for Foreigners

There are train journeys in China, and then there is Beijing to Chengdu. This is not simply a ride between two cities. It is a crossing from the flat, dry plains of northern China into the lush, subtropical heart of Sichuan province, punching straight through the Qinling Mountains that have divided north and south China for millennia. At 1,817 kilometers, this route takes you from the political capital to the culinary capital, from the Great Wall to the giant pandas, from Peking duck to Sichuan hotpot. And unlike flying, which erases the geography between two airports, the train lets you watch China transform outside your window.
Until 2017, this journey meant 14 or more hours on a conventional train. Then China opened the Xi'an-Chengdu high-speed line, an extraordinary feat of engineering that burrows through the Qinling Mountains with over 100 tunnels and dozens of soaring viaducts. Today, G-trains on the Fuxing platform cover the distance in 7 to 8 hours. It ranks among the most scenic high-speed rail journeys in the country, and we think it belongs on every China itinerary.
Route Overview
The Beijing-Chengdu high-speed route follows a roughly southwest trajectory across China. From Beijing, you speed across the North China Plain (flat wheat fields and industrial cities) for the first two hours. Around the four-hour mark, you pull into Xi'an North Station (西安北站), the ancient capital where the Terracotta Warriors stand guard. This is the midpoint, and it is where the ride gets interesting.
South of Xi'an, the train enters the Qinling Mountains (秦岭), the geographic boundary between northern and southern China. The Qinling range is not just a line on a map. It is where the climate, vegetation, cuisine, and dialect all shift. For the next two hours, your train alternates between long tunnels bored through mountain ridges and brief, breathtaking stretches across deep valleys on elevated viaducts. Keep your eyes on the window. The moment you emerge from one particular tunnel and see the landscape change from dry, brown hillsides to terraced green rice paddies, you will understand why the Chinese say "the Qinling divides heaven and earth."
By hour six or seven, you descend into the Sichuan Basin, flat again but now subtropical, humid, and intensely green. Bamboo groves, tea plantations, and farmland rush past until you glide into Chengdu.
Why train over flying? Flights from Beijing to Chengdu take about three hours in the air, but factor in the taxi to the airport, the two-hour early arrival, the security lines, the potential delays, and the taxi from the airport on the other end, and your total travel time is often 6 to 7 hours. The G-train takes 7 to 8 hours city center to city center, with none of the airport hassle. You arrive relaxed instead of frazzled. And you get to watch one of the world's great geographic transitions unfold through your window. Compare that to the Beijing to Shanghai route , which is faster but runs through flat terrain the entire way. The flight shows you nothing but clouds.

Which Stations to Know
Beijing West Station (北京西站) is your departure point for all Chengdu-bound G-trains. It is one of Beijing's largest stations, served by Metro Lines 7 and 9. Beijing West is busy and sprawling, so we recommend arriving at least 45 minutes before departure. The station has clear English signage, but the sheer size of the place means you need time to find your waiting hall, pass through security and ticket checks, and walk to your platform. There are convenience stores and fast food outlets inside, but eat before you arrive if you can.
Chengdu East Station (成都东站) is where most G-trains terminate. This is Chengdu's main high-speed terminal, located about 8 kilometers east of the city center. It connects to Metro Lines 2 and 7, making onward travel easy. From the station, Metro Line 2 will take you to Chunxi Road (春熙路), the heart of the city, in about 20 minutes.
Chengdu Station (成都站) , also known locally as Chengdu North Station (成都北站), is the older station closer to the city center on Metro Line 1. It has been closed to passengers for reconstruction since October 11, 2022, and is expected to reopen in 2027; the conventional trains that used it moved mainly to Chengdu West Station. If you are taking a G-train, you will almost certainly arrive at Chengdu East.
Our recommendation: take a G-train arriving at Chengdu East, then hop on Metro Line 2 to Chunxi Road or Tianfu Square (天府广场) to reach the central hotel district.
Train Types and Schedule
G-trains (高铁, high-speed): These cover Beijing to Chengdu in 7 to 8 hours, traveling at speeds up to 300 km/h on the Beijing-Xi'an section and slightly slower through the mountainous Xi'an-Chengdu stretch. All G-trains stop at Xi'an North (西安北) around the four-hour mark, with a brief 5 to 10 minute platform stop.
One important detail: there are only 6 to 8 direct G-trains daily on this route, significantly fewer than the Beijing-Shanghai corridor which runs dozens per day. Departures cluster in the morning, mostly between 7:00 and 10:00 AM, with occasional afternoon services. This limited schedule means tickets sell out faster, especially during holidays and peak travel seasons. Book early.
Overnight conventional train (普通列车): The Z49/Z50 still runs between Beijing and Chengdu, taking approximately 26 hours. It departs in the evening and arrives the following night. Soft sleeper berths are available. We would only recommend this to dedicated train enthusiasts or travelers on a strict budget. After experiencing the G-train through the Qinling Mountains, spending an extra 18 hours on a slower train that follows a different, less scenic route feels like a tough sell. If you do go this route, read our tips on sleeping well on overnight trains .
Ticket Prices and Booking
Current G-train prices for Beijing to Chengdu:
Second Class (二等座): approximately 778 yuan ($107)

First Class (一等座): approximately 1,245 yuan ($171)

Business Class (商务座): approximately 2,330 yuan ($320), available on select services

For the overnight Z49/Z50:
Soft Sleeper (软卧): approximately 650 yuan ($89)

Hard Sleeper (硬卧): approximately 400 yuan ($55)

Booking strategy: We strongly recommend buying tickets 7 to 10 days in advance. China's rail booking system, 12306 , opens sales 15 days before departure. Because this route has fewer daily services than major corridors like Beijing-Shanghai, seats disappear quickly. Peak seasons to watch out for: March through May (prime time for the Chengdu Panda Base, when baby pandas are most active), the October Golden Week holiday, and September through November (Sichuan's best weather).
How to compare with flights: A Beijing-Chengdu flight typically costs 600 to 1,200 yuan depending on how far ahead you book. On paper, that looks cheaper than First Class rail. But add airport transfers on both ends (easily 100-200 yuan total), the time spent in airports, and the unpredictability of flight delays, and the train becomes competitive, especially for the morning departure crowd. You leave Beijing at 7 AM and arrive in central Chengdu by 3 PM, ready to explore.
You can purchase tickets through the official 12306 app (Chinese interface, accepts foreign passports), or through English-language booking platforms like Trip.com or China Highlights. A small service fee of 20-40 yuan applies on third-party platforms, but the convenience pays for itself if you are not comfortable navigating Chinese-language apps.
Onboard Experience
Eight hours is a long ride, so comfort matters.
Second Class seats are the standard choice: clean, comfortable, with generous legroom by global standards. Seats recline moderately, there is a fold-down tray, and each seat has a power outlet beneath it. For a journey under four hours, Second Class is perfectly fine. For eight hours, it depends on your tolerance. You will be sitting upright for most of the day.
First Class gives you a wider seat, more recline, and fewer passengers per car. On a journey this long, the extra 467 yuan ($64) buys meaningful comfort. If your budget allows, we think the upgrade pays for itself.
The Qinling scenery: The highlight of the onboard experience is the stretch south of Xi'an, roughly hours 4 through 6. As the train enters the Qinling Mountains, you will notice the terrain change dramatically. The train plunges into tunnel after tunnel, some lasting several minutes, and each time it emerges, you get a fleeting window into deep mountain valleys, terraced hillsides, and occasionally a river gorge far below the viaduct. The scenery is not continuous like a Swiss alpine route; it comes in dramatic bursts between the darkness of the tunnels. Sit on the left side (seats A and B) for slightly better views on most services, though this varies.
Watch for the transition moment: somewhere between Xi'an and Hanzhong, the landscape shifts from the dry, brownish terrain of northern China to the vivid green of the subtropical south. It happens surprisingly quickly. One tunnel, and suddenly everything is different. This is the Qinling-Huaihe Line, the geographical division of China that you can actually see with your own eyes.
Food and drink: The dining car (餐车) serves hot meals in the 35 to 60 yuan range. Expect rice with a meat dish, nothing exceptional but decent enough. Attendants also push carts through the aisles selling snacks, instant noodles, drinks, and fruit. Hot water dispensers are available at the end of each car, so bring tea bags if you like. We recommend packing some of your own snacks for the journey: dried fruit, nuts, or bread from a Beijing convenience store.
Connectivity: Charge your phone before boarding and bring a cable. Cellular signal drops completely in the Qinling tunnels, which means streaming does not work reliably during the most scenic section. Download entertainment in advance. Wi-Fi on Chinese high-speed trains exists in theory but is unreliable in practice.
The Xi'an stop: Around the four-hour mark, the train pulls into Xi'an North Station for a brief stop. This is your chance to stand, stretch, and walk up and down the aisle. The stop lasts 5 to 10 minutes. Do not exit the train — there is no time to visit the platform and re-board safely.
Breaking the Journey in Xi'an
One of the best things about this route is that it passes through Xi'an (西安), one of China's most important historical cities. Rather than sitting on a single train for eight hours, consider splitting the trip.
The strategy: Buy two separate tickets — Beijing to Xi'an (approximately 4.5 hours, 515 yuan Second Class) and Xi'an to Chengdu (approximately 3 to 4 hours, 263 yuan Second Class). Spend one or two nights in Xi'an in between. The combined ticket cost is roughly the same as a direct Beijing-Chengdu ticket, and you get to see the Terracotta Warriors, walk the ancient City Wall, and eat your way through the Muslim Quarter.
Why this works so well: The Xi'an-Chengdu section is the most scenic part of the entire journey. By breaking the trip, you arrive in Xi'an refreshed, spend a day or two exploring, and then take the shorter, more spectacular train through the Qinling Mountains to Chengdu. You see the best scenery without the fatigue of an eight-hour marathon.
A popular itinerary: Many travelers we talk to follow this route: Beijing (2-3 days) to Xi'an by train (stay 2 days for Terracotta Warriors, City Wall, and Muslim Quarter) to Chengdu by train (stay 3 days for pandas, hotpot, and tea houses) to fly back to Beijing or onward. This three-city loop is one of the strongest itineraries in China, and the train connections make it seamless.
If you break the journey, note that Xi'an North Station (西安北站) is the high-speed station, located about 20 kilometers north of the city center. Metro Line 2 connects it to the Bell Tower area in about 40 minutes. Xi'an Station (西安站), the older station near the city wall, serves conventional trains.
Arrival in Chengdu
You step off the train at Chengdu East Station (成都东站) and Sichuan hits you immediately. The air is softer, warmer, and more humid than Beijing. Welcome to the land of pandas, hotpot, and afternoon mahjong.
Getting to your hotel: Metro Line 2 from Chengdu East takes you to the city center in about 20 minutes. For Chunxi Road (春熙路, the main pedestrian shopping area and a popular hotel district), ride Line 2 to Chunxi Road station. For Tianfu Square (天府广场, near museums and government buildings), it is one stop further. A taxi or ride-hailing car (use Didi, China's Uber equivalent) to central Chengdu costs 30 to 50 yuan and takes 20 to 30 minutes depending on traffic.
Your first evening, hotpot: You have just crossed China from north to south. You are in the hotpot capital of the world. This is not optional. Head to a local hotpot restaurant for dinner. Xiaolongkan (小龙坎) has branches everywhere and is reliably excellent for first-timers. The numbingly spicy (麻辣) broth is their signature. Dazhai Huoguo (大寨火锅) is another strong choice with a more local atmosphere. Order the half-and-half pot (鸳鸯锅) if you want one spicy and one mild broth side by side. Budget 80 to 150 yuan per person for a full hotpot feast. Your lips will tingle. This is normal.
Day two, giant pandas: The Chengdu Research Base of Giant Panda Breeding (成都大熊猫繁育研究基地) is the city's most famous attraction, and it lives up to the hype. Take Metro Line 3 to Panda Avenue Station (熊猫大道站), then a short shuttle or taxi to the base entrance. Tickets cost 55 yuan. The critical advice: arrive before 8:30 AM. Pandas are most active during morning feeding times. By midday, they are asleep in the trees and you are fighting crowds. Early morning visitors get the best experience by a wide margin: playful cubs, active adults, and manageable paths.
Beyond the pandas: Chengdu rewards slower exploration. Visit Jinli Ancient Street (锦里古街) for evening snacks and lantern-lit atmosphere. Walk through the Wide and Narrow Alleys (宽窄巷子) for teahouses and street food. Spend an afternoon in People's Park (人民公园) watching locals play cards, practice tai chi, and drink tea. This is Chengdu's famous laid-back lifestyle in action. If you have an extra day, take a day trip to Leshan to see the 71-meter Giant Buddha carved into a riverside cliff, reachable by high-speed train in under an hour.
Practical Tips for the Journey
Pack smart for eight hours. Bring a neck pillow, snacks, a full water bottle, downloaded entertainment, and a phone charger. Dress in layers. Train cars can be aggressively air-conditioned.
Passport ready. You will need your passport to buy tickets and to pass through station security. Keep it accessible, not buried in your checked luggage.
Luggage is manageable. There are overhead racks and space behind the last row of seats in each car for larger bags. China's train luggage rules allow 20 kg per adult, and on high-speed trains each item must be no more than 130 cm in length, width and height combined. If you cannot lift it, it is too heavy.
Learn a few phrases. Station announcements are in Mandarin and English on G-trains. But knowing basic phrases like "Beijing Xi Zhan" (北京西站, Beijing West Station) and "Chengdu Dong Zhan" (成都东站, Chengdu East Station) helps when asking for directions.
Time zone note. All of China runs on a single time zone (Beijing Time, UTC+8), so there is no adjustment when traveling from Beijing to Chengdu despite covering nearly 2,000 kilometers.
This route was impossible at this speed just a few years ago. Now it cuts straight through mountains that have shaped the country's history for thousands of years. Book your tickets, pack your snacks, and watch the landscape tell the story of China as you cross it.
