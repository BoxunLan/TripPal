A 澡堂 (zǎo táng) is not a spa. It is a bathing hall where a retired factory worker, a student and a travelling salesman all take their clothes off, shower properly, and then sit in hot water for an hour.
Nobody there will mind that you are foreign. What they will mind is if you get into the pool without showering first. That is the only rule you genuinely cannot get wrong, and the rest of this is the order everything happens in, so that walking in does not feel like an exam.
The sequence
Pay at the front counter — a flat entry fee. Some chains log ID, so have your passport. You get a waterproof wristband and a locker key.

Men and women split here: 男 is men, 女 is women. There is no mixed area beyond this point.

Shoes off at the locker room entrance, into the shoe rack. Street shoes do not go in.

Undress completely. Swimwear is not worn, anywhere, by anyone.

Shower with soap. A real shower, not a rinse. This is the rule.

Then the pools — hot first, cold after, as long as you like.

Optional extras are charged to the wristband, not paid in cash: a scrub, a massage, food in the rest lounge.

Settle the wristband at the desk on the way out.

Ninety minutes to two hours is a first visit: entry, shower, two cycles through the pools, the sauna, one scrub or a short massage, and out.

Two price worlds, and knowing which one you walked into saves both money and confusion. The small neighbourhood places take cash and WeChat Pay and keep it simple. The chains take Alipay and WeChat Pay , occasionally a foreign card at the front desk, and inside they run on the wristband rather than cash.
The rules that matter
The logic behind all of them: shared water stays clean, shared space stays quiet, nobody is looked at.
Shower with soap before any pool or sauna. If you forget, an attendant will wave you back — politely, and in front of everyone.

No swimwear. Everyone is naked. A swimsuit marks you as someone who did not ask.

No phones in the wet area. Not for photographs, not to check the time. The phone stays in the locker.

Quiet is the default. People are reading, dozing, or having very slow conversations.

Things that are not problems: not speaking Chinese — the whole process is visual and gestures work. Being visibly nervous — everyone expects that of a first-timer. Tattoos — a decade ago some bathhouses refused entry, and that has largely gone.
What to bring
A small dry bag for your phone and hotel key.

A hair tie if your hair is long; some places require it in the pool.

¥200–300 in cash as a fallback.

Not a swimsuit. Genuinely.

Not jewellery, and not your main wallet unless the place asks for ID.

Towels are sometimes a ¥5–10 rental. Shampoo, body wash and conditioner are in bulk dispensers; razors and combs are single-use.

Walk

Start with a chain if it is your first time. The signage is clearer, you will not be the only stranger in the room, and it costs two to three times the neighbourhood rate for the privilege. You do miss the part where old men argue about property prices in the hot pool, which is arguably the point.
To find one: search 洗浴中心 near your hotel in Amap or Baidu Maps, filter for 4.5 and above, and read the comments with a translation app.
Many Hangzhou complexes run 24 hours and let people sleep in lounge chairs or nap rooms — travellers occasionally use them as a cheap night's rest between trains.
If it is too much on week one
Gym day passes are the softer version. Supermonkey (超级猩猩) runs ¥79–129 depending on the class, Keep Land ¥60–120, Pure Fitness ¥150–250 at the Kerry Centre. Chinese gym showers are individual cubicles with doors, exactly like a Western gym.
The trade-off is honest: you have used a bathroom, not had the experience. But it is a reasonable way to get clean while you work up to the real thing.
Going as a couple
The bathing halls are strictly single-gender, so you cannot soak together. What you can do is meet afterwards in the shared rest lounge, which at the larger complexes is a full common area with food, recliners and television — everyone dressed in the house pyjamas the place issues.
Elsewhere in the city: the Grand Canal water bus and the temple trail above West Lake both pair well with an afternoon in hot water. Hangzhou is 45 minutes from Shanghai by high-speed rail .
FAQ
Can women visit a Chinese public bathhouse alone?
Yes. Women's sections are entirely separate, often on a different floor, and staffed by female attendants. Solo female visitors are unremarkable.
Do I have to be naked?
In the bathing area, yes — everybody is, and swimwear is not worn. In the shared rest lounge you wear the pyjamas the bathhouse issues.
What does a Hangzhou bathhouse cost?
¥30–50 entry at a neighbourhood zǎo táng and ¥50–80 at a chain. A two-hour visit with a scrub runs ¥90 to ¥280 at the small places and ¥200 to ¥500 at the chains.
Are tattoos a problem?
Generally not in 2026. A decade ago some bathhouses refused entry to visible tattoos; that has largely faded.
Do I tip?
Not required — staff are on wages. ¥10–20 in cash to a scrub or massage attendant is appreciated but nobody expects it.
