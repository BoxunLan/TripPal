Is YouTube Blocked in China? The Short Answer
Yes, YouTube is blocked across mainland China, and it stays blocked in 2026. The Great Firewall has cut it off since 2009, and nothing about that has loosened. Open the app or the website on a normal Chinese connection and you get a permanent spinning wheel or a timeout. It is not slow, it is unreachable.
This is the same wall that blocks the rest of Google, including Gmail, Google Maps, Google Drive, and search, plus WhatsApp, Instagram, and Facebook. They are not throttled or restricted. On a standard connection inside the mainland they simply do not load. If your trip depends on any of them, you need a workaround in place before you land.
One geographic note worth knowing: Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan are outside the Great Firewall. YouTube works normally there with no setup at all. The block only applies to the mainland, so a layover in Hong Kong is not a useful test of whether your phone is ready for Shanghai or Beijing.

Why Streaming Video Is the Hardest Test of Any Workaround
Plenty of bypass tools can sneak a text message or load a low-bandwidth page. Video is a different problem entirely. YouTube is heavy, sustained traffic, and that is exactly what China's filtering is best at catching and slowing down. The Great Firewall uses deep packet inspection, which examines the shape of your traffic rather than just where it is going. A steady high-bandwidth stream is easy to flag, and once flagged it gets throttled or dropped.
This is why a VPN that seems to work for checking email can collapse the moment you press play on a video. The connection holds, but the throughput gets squeezed below anything watchable. Long-term users report that older VPN protocols now get quality-of-service throttled down to a crawl that is technically connected but unusable for streaming. Evenings are the worst window, when international gateway congestion stacks on top of heavier filtering and peak user load. The result is endless buffering at the exact hour you sit down to watch.
So the real question is not whether a tool can reach YouTube once. It is whether it can hold a smooth stream for thirty minutes at 9pm. That is the bar video sets, and it is a bar a struggling VPN often cannot clear in 2026.
VPN vs Roaming eSIM: Why the eSIM Wins for Video
A VPN can still work in China, but it has become increasingly unreliable since the April 2026 crackdown. That month, the three Chinese carriers began blocking VPN-signature IP addresses on detection with no grace period, part of a coordinated push that also pulled relay servers offline. A VPN that worked last year may go dark mid-trip, and the brands that hold up tend to be the ones with heavy traffic obfuscation. We would not assert a single winner as fact, but the ones reported by long-term users to survive are the exception, not the rule, and even those wobble under video load.
A roaming eSIM works on a completely different principle, and that is why it is the most reliable 2026 fix. Its mobile data exits through a foreign network, typically routed via Hong Kong or Singapore, and never touches the filtered Chinese internet. There is no VPN to detect, no app to install, no protocol to fingerprint. YouTube, Google Maps, and WhatsApp just open the way they do at home. Because the bypass happens at the network level instead of fighting the firewall, streaming stays smooth where a VPN buffers. The honest trade-off: an eSIM costs money per gigabyte and a VPN is often a flat subscription. But for video, the eSIM is paying for the one thing a struggling VPN cannot guarantee, which is bandwidth that actually holds. Set Everything Up Before You Land
This is the step most travelers skip, and it is the one that strands them. Once you are inside mainland China, the tools you need to get online are themselves blocked. VPN provider websites are filtered, and the Apple App Store and Google Play in China either hide or restrict the apps you want. If you arrive with nothing installed, you are stuck trying to download a fix through the very wall you are trying to climb.
Do all of it on home WiFi before your flight. Install and test your VPN, or buy and activate your roaming eSIM and confirm it connects. An eSIM is especially convenient here because you can install the profile in advance and switch it on the moment you land, with no shop visit and no app download required on arrival. Walk off the plane already connected.
Treat this as a pre-departure checklist item, not something to figure out at the airport. The Beijing and Shanghai airport WiFi is behind the same firewall as everywhere else, so it will not bail you out.
The WiFi Gap That Catches People Out
Here is the trap that surprises eSIM users. A roaming eSIM bypasses the Great Firewall only on its own mobile data. The instant you connect to hotel, café, airport, or restaurant WiFi, your traffic routes back through Chinese infrastructure and gets filtered again. The eSIM is sitting right there in your phone, but if you are on WiFi, it is not the connection carrying your traffic.
People hit this constantly. They sit down in the hotel lobby, the phone auto-joins the WiFi, and suddenly YouTube stops loading even though it worked fine on the street five minutes ago. Nothing broke. The phone just quietly switched networks.
The fix is simple: when you want unfiltered access, turn WiFi off and stay on your eSIM data. If you are pairing an eSIM with a VPN instead, then the VPN does need to be running on that hotel WiFi to punch through. Know which connection you are on, because it determines whether you are over the wall or back behind it.
What About Maps and Other Blocked Apps?
YouTube is rarely the only thing travelers miss, and the same logic applies across the board. Google Maps is blocked too, and it is a special headache because even when you force it through a VPN, the map tiles are shifted by China's GCJ-02 coordinate system, leaving your blue dot hundreds of meters off the road you are standing on. It is not just blocked, it is wrong.
The cleaner answer for navigation is to stop fighting it. On iPhone, Apple Maps quietly uses Amap data inside China and works with no VPN at all, as long as your phone's region is set to something other than China. The local apps Amap and Baidu Maps also work natively and are far more accurate than Google here. We cover the full setup in our guide to the best map app in China . For everything else that is blocked, a roaming eSIM or a working VPN restores it the same way it restores YouTube.
Chinese Alternatives: Bilibili and Youku
If you would rather watch what locals watch, China has its own video giants that need no workaround at all. Bilibili is the closest thing to YouTube, built around creator-led, longer-form content with a famously active community and scrolling on-screen comments. Youku and iQIYI cover the long-form and licensed-show side, closer to a Netflix-style catalog.
These work natively inside China with no VPN, since they live behind the firewall rather than outside it. The catch for foreign visitors is language: the interfaces and most content are in Chinese, with only limited English around settings, so they are more of a curiosity than a full YouTube replacement. They are worth a look if you are interested in Chinese creators or shows, but if you specifically want your own YouTube subscriptions, channels, and playlists, you are back to the eSIM or VPN route.
FAQ
Can I watch YouTube in China with a VPN?
Sometimes, but it has become unreliable since the April 2026 crackdown, when carriers began blocking VPN IPs on detection. Even when a VPN connects, bandwidth-heavy video often buffers or drops because the firewall throttles streaming traffic. A roaming eSIM is the more dependable choice for video.
Why does YouTube buffer even when my VPN is connected in China?
China's deep packet inspection targets heavy, sustained traffic like video streams, throttling them even when the connection technically holds. Older VPN protocols get squeezed to unusable speeds, and evenings between 8 and 11pm are the worst due to gateway congestion and peak filtering.
Does a roaming eSIM let me use YouTube in China without a VPN?
Yes. A roaming eSIM routes your mobile data through a foreign network outside mainland China, so it never hits the Great Firewall. YouTube, Google, and WhatsApp open normally with no VPN and no app. Just keep WiFi off, since WiFi routes back through filtered Chinese infrastructure.
Do I need to set up YouTube access before arriving in China?
Yes, absolutely. VPN websites and the relevant app downloads are blocked once you are inside mainland China, so you cannot fix it on arrival. Install and test your VPN, or activate your roaming eSIM, on home WiFi before your flight.
Does YouTube work in Hong Kong, Macau, or Taiwan?
Yes. All three sit outside the Great Firewall, so YouTube and other Google services work normally there with no VPN or eSIM needed. The block applies only to mainland China, so a Hong Kong layover does not confirm your phone is ready for the mainland.
What is the Chinese equivalent of YouTube?
Bilibili is the closest match, focused on creator-led, longer-form video with an active community, while Youku and iQIYI handle licensed shows. They work inside China without a VPN, but the interfaces and content are mostly in Chinese, so they do not fully replace your own YouTube account.
Video is the honest stress test, and it is why the eSIM-versus-VPN question is not a tie here: a VPN that loads a web page acceptably can still be unwatchable at 1080p.
