Yunnan grows the tea leaves that built the Tea Horse Road, and almost every Pu'er cake sold in China traces back to a village within a day's drive of Kunming. If you want to buy real Pu'er, Dian hong, or moonlight white and actually take it home, you have two serious markets: Kunming's Xiongda Tea Market and Kangle Tea City, plus a handful of trustworthy shops tucked into Lijiang Old Town and Shuhe. Everything else is either overpriced, fake, or a pour-and-pressure scam wearing a nice smile.
This guide breaks down the tea, the cities, the prices for 2026, and the exact red flags that separate a real vendor from a tourist trap.

Yunnan Teas Worth Buying in 2026
Yunnan's tea output is enormous, but four categories matter for travelers. Know them before you walk into a market or you will end up with whatever the vendor wants to move that week.
Pu'er (Sheng and Shu)
Pu'er is Yunnan's flagship. It comes in two styles. Sheng (raw) Pu'er is pressed from sun-dried large-leaf varietals and aged naturally, sometimes for decades. Fresh sheng is bright, astringent, grassy, and a bit bitter. Well-aged sheng mellows into honey, dried fruit, and wood. Shu (ripe) Pu'er is fermented in a wet pile for 45 to 60 days, which compresses years of aging into weeks. It tastes earthy, smooth, and dark, with notes of wet forest floor and dates.
Both styles are usually pressed into a 357g disc called a bing (pronounced "bing"), wrapped in cotton paper, and stacked in bamboo tongs of seven cakes. You will also see tuo cha (bowl shape, 100-250g), brick, and mushroom shapes.
Dian Hong
Dian hong is Yunnan black tea. The best grades are made from golden tips, pure buds with no stems, and produce a soup that looks like polished copper. Taste runs to malt, cocoa, and baked sweet potato. Dian hong is friendlier to Western palates than sheng Pu'er and travels home without needing aging.
Moonlight White
A lightly oxidized white tea with silver-furred leaves that look almost black on one side. Delicate, floral, with a long honey finish. Harder to find at fair prices outside Yunnan.
Snow Chrysanthemum and Rose Buds
Not technically tea, but everywhere in Yunnan markets. Kunlun snow chrysanthemum from the northwest is a gift-box staple, and Lijiang is big on rose buds for herbal blends. Fine as gifts, not what you came for.
Kunming: Where Serious Tea Gets Sold
Kunming is the wholesale brain of the Yunnan tea trade. Two markets matter, and a third is where most tourists unfortunately end up.
Xiongda Tea Market
Xiongda (often written Xiong Da) is the single biggest tea market in Kunming and arguably in all of China. Hundreds of stalls fill multiple buildings, organized roughly by tea type and origin region. Wholesale buyers from Guangzhou, Beijing, and Hong Kong fly in to source cakes here. Retail-friendly stalls sit on the ground floors closest to the main entrances.
Get there with Kunming Metro Line 2 or a Didi ride; most vendors will let you taste four or five cakes before committing. Bring small bills if you plan to cash-pay, but Alipay and WeChat Pay work everywhere. A 357g sheng cake from a named mountain (Bulang, Banzhang, Yiwu) will run ¥150-500 at wholesale friendly prices. Decent daily-drinker shu cakes start around ¥60-120. Factory tea from Menghai or Xiaguan, with proof of origin wrapper, usually sits at ¥80-250.
Kangle Tea City
Kangle Tea City is the other big market, more compact than Xiongda and easier to navigate as a first-timer. It skews slightly more retail, with nicer tasting rooms and more English signage. Expect to pay roughly 10-20 percent more than Xiongda for equivalent tea, but the shopping experience is calmer.
Jinma Biji Walking Street (Avoid for Tea)
Jinma Biji is the touristy pedestrian drag near the city center. The tea shops here are the most expensive in Kunming for the worst quality, often repackaging mediocre leaves with fake vintage dates. Buy snacks here; buy tea elsewhere.
Getting to Xiongda by metro: take Metro Line 2 to Baiyun Lu (白云路) and transfer to a local bus, or ride to Jinxing (金星) station, then walk or grab a short taxi. The market sits at the corner of Jinshi Road and the North Second Ring (二环北路) in Panlong District, roughly 10 km from the city center. Locals sum up the city's two big markets in one phrase: north Xiongda, south Kangle. If you are staying downtown, Kangle in the south is usually the easier ride; Xiongda in the north is the larger and more atmosphere-heavy of the two.
What free tasting actually means here: shop owners will brew you cup after cup, sometimes ten different teas in one sitting, at no cost and with no obligation to buy. This is the normal way the market works, not a favor you owe back. You can taste at three or four shops, leave each one empty-handed, and nobody will be offended. Most sellers speak limited English, so save the Chinese names of the teas you want (生普, 熟普, 滇红, 古树) on your phone and point. Shops will happily sell as little as 100 grams, so you do not have to commit to a full 357g cake to try something.
Lijiang: Buying Tea in the Old Town and Shuhe
Lijiang is not a wholesale hub, but the Old Town has legitimate tea shops catering to travelers who do not want to backtrack to Kunming. Prices run 20-40 percent above Kunming wholesale for comparable quality. That is the convenience tax.
Lijiang Old Town (Dayan)
Walk away from Sifang Street toward the quieter side lanes near the Mu Family Mansion. Look for shops with visible aged cakes on shelves and a tasting table where the owner is actually drinking tea with locals, not a hostess smiling at every passing foreigner. The good shops let you taste three or four cakes without pressure.
Shuhe Old Town
Shuhe, ten minutes by taxi north of Dayan, is calmer and has a handful of serious tea sellers supplying the Naxi hotels. Prices are similar to Old Town Lijiang, but the atmosphere is less chaotic.
What to Pay in Lijiang
Expect ¥80-200 for a drinkable shu cake, ¥200-600 for a named-mountain sheng from 2019-2023, and ¥400-1000 for a well-regarded aged cake 10 or more years old. Anything claiming to be pre-2005 or "Banzhang lao shu" for under ¥1000 is lying.
Why locals steer you out of Dayan: Chinese consumer guides estimate that more than 60% of tea shops inside Lijiang Old Town misrepresent a cake's age or origin. A widely cited example: a tourist paid 300 RMB for an "ancient-tree" cake on Wuyi Street (五一街) that later proved to be ordinary tea costing roughly 30 RMB. The rule of thumb Chinese buyers repeat is that genuine ancient-tree (古树) tea is never cheap, so a 30 to 50 RMB "古树" cake is, in their words, a tax on naivety. Treat "limited edition" and "collector's grade" labels in the Old Town as marketing, not grading.
Shuhe (束河), about 4 km from Dayan, is where Chinese visitors are told to buy instead. Rents are lower, foot traffic is calmer, and shops are less geared toward one-time tourists, so you get more honest prices and less pressure. For a more reliable buy inside Dayan itself, head to the dedicated tea shops on Qiyi Street (七一街) rather than the stalls clustered on the main tourist drags.
Sheng vs Shu Pu'er: Grading and Wrapper Codes
Buying Pu'er blind is hard. A few checkpoints will save you from the worst mistakes.
Grade and Vintage Claims
Factory Pu'er uses a four-digit code on the wrapper. The first two digits are the recipe year, the third is the leaf grade (1 fine, 9 coarse), and the fourth is the factory number (1 Kunming, 2 Menghai, 3 Xiaguan, 4 others). A wrapper reading "7542" means a 1975 Menghai Factory recipe using grade-4 leaves. The year on the wrapper is the recipe, not the production year, which should be stamped or inked separately.
Sheng Pu'er Quick Tests
Fresh sheng smells like hay, sunlight, and sometimes stone fruit. The dry leaves should be dark green to brown-green, whole, with visible buds. The wet leaves after brewing should be supple, not brittle. Soup color runs pale yellow to gold. If it tastes smoky (beyond a hint), astringent without any sweetness, or like wet newspaper, walk away.
Shu Pu'er Quick Tests
Good shu smells earthy but clean, never musty or mushroom-like in a bad way. Dry leaves are dark brown to near black. Soup is deep red-brown, almost black, and should be clear, not muddy. A well-made shu has a thick, smooth mouthfeel; a bad one tastes like dirt and leaves your tongue rough.
The fastest sniff test for fake "aged" or "ancient-tree" claims: ask the seller for the exact mountain or village, not just "Yunnan." A genuine vendor names the specific source (Banzhang, Yiwu, Jingmai, a named village). Vague answers plus a low price almost always mean plantation tea (台地茶) dressed up as gushu, or a tiny bit of real ancient-tree leaf blended into mostly ordinary leaf. Two visual tells from tea farmers: ancient trees grow scattered and irregular, so leaves vary in size and shape, while plantation tea comes off neat rows and looks uniform. And remember the price logic, genuine aged sheng from the 1980s or 1990s reaches auction prices, so a "1990s" cake sold at young-sheng money is mislabeled, badly stored, or fake.

The Tea House Pour-and-Pressure Scam
This is the single biggest risk for foreign travelers in Yunnan tea shopping. It works like this.
A friendly English-speaking woman approaches you near a tourist spot (usually Green Lake in Kunming or the Old Town gates in Lijiang) and invites you to a "traditional tea ceremony."

You are seated in a small upstairs room. A hostess pours five or six teas in small cups, talking about health benefits and her grandmother's village.

A bill appears for ¥800, ¥2000, sometimes ¥5000 for the tasting alone, plus hard pressure to buy a cake at ¥3000-10000.

A large man appears near the door when you try to leave.

How to Avoid It
Never accept a tea invitation from someone who approached you on the street.

Real tea shops have an open storefront at ground level in a market or on a shopping lane. They do not recruit foreigners into upstairs rooms.

Tastings in legitimate markets are free. A vendor who charges to taste is already signaling a scam.

If pricing is not clearly posted or offered in writing before the pour, leave.

If You Are Already in the Room
Stay calm. Say firmly in English or via a translation app that you will pay only for the water consumed, offer ¥50-100, and walk to the door. Keep your phone recording audio. If physically blocked, call 110 (police) and say "jing cha" (police). The scammers almost always back down when police are mentioned, because the shop is usually running unregistered.
The version Chinese guides flag most: an over-friendly host (often a "pretty boss") who can't actually explain the difference between sheng and shu, pours you tea enthusiastically, then steers you to a "reserve" or "collector" cake. One cited victim's cake brewed dark as soy sauce. The pattern to watch for is enthusiasm and flattery substituting for any real product knowledge, plus a sudden jump to a high-margin "special" cake once you seem invested.
Paying, Packing, and Getting Tea Home
Once you have a cake you trust, the last step is making sure it travels.
Payment in 2026
Alipay and WeChat Pay cover every stall in Xiongda, Kangle, and reputable Lijiang shops. Both now accept foreign cards linked to international accounts, and Tour Pass balances work for smaller amounts. Cash in ¥100 notes still works everywhere and gets you a better bargaining position.
Packing for Flights
Compressed tea cakes count as solid food and are allowed in both checked and carry-on luggage domestically and on most international flights. Vacuum-sealed bags protect cakes from humidity and odor contamination. Avoid packing tea next to strongly scented items; Pu'er absorbs smells like a sponge.
Customs at Home
Most countries allow reasonable personal quantities of tea. The US allows commercial-packaged tea in checked bags with no quantity limit for personal use. Australia and New Zealand are stricter; declare tea on arrival and expect a brief inspection. EU countries are relaxed for dried leaf tea.
A practical note on tasting and flights: tea you taste in a shop is brewed on the spot, so there is no liquid to declare, and dry compressed cakes are solid, not a liquid, so they are fine in both carry-on and checked bags. The real risk is crushing, a 357g cake survives checked luggage far better than a flimsy paper-wrapped one in carry-on getting squashed. Keep cakes in their original wrappers with any receipt or shop card; this both protects the tea and helps if a customs officer at home asks what it is.
FAQ
Is it safe for foreigners to buy Pu'er tea in Yunnan?
Yes, at legitimate markets like Xiongda Tea Market and Kangle Tea City in Kunming, or at established shops in Lijiang Old Town and Shuhe. Risk is concentrated almost entirely in street invitations to "tea ceremonies," which are the main scam vector. Public markets with open stalls are safe.
How much should a good Pu'er cake cost in 2026?
A drinkable daily shu cake runs ¥60-150 in Kunming. A named-mountain sheng cake from the past five years sits at ¥200-500. Aged sheng from 10 years back climbs to ¥400-1000. Anything claiming to be 20+ years old under ¥1000 is almost certainly fake. Lijiang adds 20-40 percent to all these numbers.
What is the difference between sheng and shu Pu'er?
Sheng (raw) Pu'er is naturally aged, tastes bright and astringent when young and mellows over years into honey and dried fruit. Shu (ripe) Pu'er is wet-pile fermented in 45-60 days, producing a dark, earthy, smooth tea right away. Sheng is for collectors and aging; shu is for drinking now.
Can I taste before I buy at a Yunnan tea market?
Yes. At Xiongda, Kangle, and any real Lijiang shop, tasting three to five cakes is normal and free. If a vendor refuses to let you taste, or demands payment for tasting, leave immediately; that is scam behavior.
Will my Pu'er cake get through airport security going home?
Yes. Compressed tea cakes are classified as solid dry goods and pass security in both carry-on and checked luggage worldwide. Keep the original cotton wrapper with its factory markings so customs officers can identify it. Declare on arrival only if your country requires plant-matter declarations (Australia, New Zealand).
The tea markets are a reason to slow down in Yunnan rather than a stop between flights.
