You bought a 3 kg porcelain vase in Jingdezhen, a box of tea in Hangzhou, and a rolled silk painting in Suzhou, and now your carry-on does not close. Every Chinese will tell you the same thing — just use SF Express (顺丰, shun feng) — but the moment you walk into the bright red-and-white storefront on the corner you discover that the intake form wants a Chinese ID number, the clerk does not speak English, and the payment screen defaults to a WeChat Pay account tied to a Chinese bank card. Sending a package in China as a foreigner is entirely possible in 2026, but the process is not the one your hostel front desk described.
Here is how to actually ship a box domestically or internationally without getting stuck at the counter.

The rule of thumb in 2026 is simple: SF Express for anything inside China when speed matters, and China EMS for anything leaving the country. If you are shipping a fragile souvenir home, those are the only two names you need to remember.
SF Express (顺丰)
The FedEx of China. Uniformed couriers, tracked pickups, air freight on long routes, and branches in almost every neighborhood of every Chinese city. Domestic SF deliveries typically reach their destination within 24-48 hours within a province and 48-72 hours across the country. International service covers more than 200 countries but is noticeably more expensive than EMS for parcels over 2 kg.
China EMS (中国邮政速递物流)
The dedicated express arm of the state postal service. EMS is the best option for international shipments out of China because it is tied into the Universal Postal Union treaty network, which means it hands off directly to USPS, Royal Mail, Australia Post, Deutsche Post, and most other national postal services at the destination. EMS international pricing is predictable, customs handling is standardized, and the branch staff at big post offices are used to foreign customers.
China Post (中国邮政)
The regular state postal service. Slower than EMS, much cheaper, and available at every green-and-yellow post office you see. Small parcels under 2 kg to international destinations are often 30-50 percent cheaper than EMS but take 2-6 weeks to arrive. Fine for non-urgent souvenir shipments, wrong choice for anything you actually need on a timeline.
JD Logistics (京东物流)
JD's in-house courier, used mostly for JD.com orders but available for third-party drop-offs in major cities. Fast domestic delivery, occasionally cheaper than SF on short routes. International service exists but is inconsistent for tourist drop-ins and better avoided unless you already have a JD account.
What you need at the counter
Every courier in China now requires real-name registration on parcels. This rule dates to 2015 but has been more strictly enforced since 2022 in response to anti-smuggling and fraud crackdowns. For a foreigner this means:
Your actual passport at the counter (not a photo or photocopy)

A Chinese phone number that can receive SMS for the sender registration, or the clerk's workaround phone

The recipient's full name, phone, and address in the destination country

Payment in cash, Alipay, WeChat Pay, or occasionally UnionPay — foreign Visa/Mastercard are rarely accepted at parcel counters

An open, unsealed box so the clerk can visually inspect the contents before you close and tape it

Point 5 is the one that catches tourists off guard. You cannot bring a pre-sealed box and drop it off. The counter clerk is legally required to verify the contents before accepting the parcel, and they will open any sealed box or ask you to open it.
The phone number issue is solvable. SF Express clerks and major post offices will usually register the parcel under the storefront's phone number and simply give you the waybill number for tracking. Some branches also have a tourist workflow that registers the parcel against your passport number instead of a phone number. Ask for 外国人寄件 (wai guo ren ji jian), which means "foreigner shipping," and most clerks will know the workflow.

Prohibited items
Chinese couriers will reject or confiscate a long list of items at the counter, and customs on the destination side will catch anything that slipped through. The most common tourist-related problems in 2026:
Lithium batteries over 100 Wh — cannot be shipped internationally at all, and loose cells under 100 Wh need approved packaging

Power banks — same rules as lithium batteries, routinely confiscated

Liquids over 500 ml — alcohol, sauces, oils, perfumes all hit this limit

Tea in loose leaf form over 2 kg — allowed domestically, regulated internationally

Traditional Chinese medicine — banned from many international destinations due to CITES wildlife protection rules

Antiques older than 100 years — cannot be legally exported without a cultural relics permit

Fresh food, meat, dairy, eggs — banned internationally

Cash, credit cards, passports — cannot be shipped

Aerosols and compressed gas — cannot be shipped internationally

The CITES rule is the one that catches tourists most often. Tiger balm is fine. Anything containing ingredients like rhinoceros horn, pangolin, or bear bile is criminally banned in most Western countries and will be destroyed at customs even if somehow accepted at the Chinese counter.
How to actually ship souvenirs home
The three most common tourist shipments out of China are tea, silk, and porcelain. Each has specific handling rules.
Tea
Loose leaf tea in factory-sealed packaging ships fine. Loose tea in paper bags should be repackaged into a proper plastic pouch or the box will stink of leaves on arrival. On the customs form, describe it accurately as "tea, leaves, dried, for personal consumption" and declare its real value — under-declaring to dodge duty is illegal and can get the package held. Some countries (Australia, New Zealand) have strict biosecurity rules and require tea to be commercially packaged.
Silk
Silk scarves, ties, and small textiles ship without issue. Larger silk paintings and framed works need a rigid tube or flat cardboard to prevent creasing. Use SF Express rather than EMS for silk over ¥1,000 in declared value because SF handles textiles more gently. Declare at actual value; underreporting silk value is a common tourist mistake that causes customs issues in the destination country.
Porcelain
The most fragile and most commonly damaged tourist shipment. Pack in a double-walled box with at least 5 cm of bubble wrap on every side, and mark the box as fragile with the Chinese word 易碎 (yi sui). SF Express has an explicit "fragile packaging service" at larger branches that costs ¥30-60 extra and includes professional bubble-wrap and foam inserts. Always pay for it when shipping ceramics.
Numbered steps for a typical SF Express tourist shipment
Here is what a normal 3 kg international shipment actually looks like from the moment you walk into an SF Express storefront:
Walk in with your unsealed parcel and your passport

Tell the clerk the destination country in English; they will usually understand "America," "UK," "Germany," "Japan," or "Australia"

The clerk places your parcel on the scale to weigh it

The clerk opens your box and visually inspects the contents for prohibited items

The clerk hands you a paper waybill or asks you to fill in the recipient details on a tablet

You write or type the recipient's full name, street address, city, postal code, phone number, and country

The clerk scans your passport for the sender real-name registration

The clerk quotes the total price and payment options

You pay with Alipay, WeChat Pay, or cash

The clerk seals the box in front of you, prints the waybill label, and attaches it

You receive a tracking number and the estimated delivery date

The entire process takes 10-20 minutes for an experienced clerk and 30-45 minutes if you are in a small-town branch that has rarely handled a foreign customer.
Customs and import duties on the receiving end
Customs rules on the receiving end changed sharply in 2025, so do not assume a small package slips through duty-free. The big one is the United States: it scrapped its old USD 800 "de minimis" duty-free allowance — that ended for parcels from China on 2 May 2025, was suspended for every country on 29 August 2025, and is still suspended in 2026. In practice almost any package arriving in the US now clears formal customs and can be charged duty regardless of value. Other countries still have low-value thresholds, but they are shrinking: the UK clears goods under GBP 135 of customs duty (import VAT is still charged), the EU under EUR 150 (VAT applies from the first euro, and a flat EUR 3 per-parcel charge starts 1 July 2026), Australia assesses GST and duty above AUD 1,000, and Canada charges on most things above CAD 20. Assume a gift sent home will likely attract some tax or fee on arrival.
Two common mistakes:
Splitting one big shipment into multiple small ones on the same day can be flagged as duty evasion

Under-declaring value on the customs form is technically illegal in most countries and can cause the whole package to be seized

Declare honestly, keep receipts, and expect to pay roughly 10-20 percent in duties and tax on anything your home customs assesses — and for US-bound parcels, assume that is now any package, since the de minimis allowance is gone.
FAQ
Can foreigners send packages in China without a Chinese ID?
Yes. SF Express, China EMS, and China Post all accept foreign passports at the counter. The clerk will register the parcel under your passport number (or occasionally under the storefront's phone number as a workaround for SMS verification). You do not need a Chinese ID card.
Which is cheaper for international shipping, SF Express or China EMS?
EMS is consistently cheaper than SF Express for international parcels over 2 kg going to the USA, UK, and Europe. SF Express is slightly cheaper or comparable to EMS for very small parcels under 1 kg and for nearby Asian destinations like Japan, Singapore, and Korea. For the typical souvenir shipment of 3-5 kg home to Europe or North America, EMS wins on price by 20-30 percent.
How do I ship something home from China without speaking Chinese?
Use EMS at a major post office, or SF Express at a branch inside a large shopping mall, department store, or hotel. Both have staff who handle foreign customers regularly in big cities. Bring your passport, an unsealed box, the recipient's full address written in English, and pre-loaded Alipay or WeChat Pay. Ask for 国际快递 (guo ji kuai di), which means "international express."
Can I send tea, silk, and porcelain home as souvenirs?
Yes, all three are routinely shipped internationally from China without issues. Pack porcelain in double-walled boxes with bubble wrap, keep silk flat or rolled in a tube, and use sealed packaging for tea. Declare at accurate value and be aware that some countries (Australia, New Zealand) have strict biosecurity rules for tea and plant matter.
What is the fastest way to send a package from China to the USA?
SF Express International Express typically delivers to major US cities in 4-7 days, while EMS takes 6-12 days. SF Express is roughly 50 percent more expensive than EMS for the same package. For anything time-sensitive like documents or gifts needed within a week, SF Express wins. For typical souvenir shipments without a deadline, EMS is the better value.
Shipping is usually the answer to a luggage problem you created shopping, so it is worth knowing the limits you are shipping around: what you can carry on a Chinese train is stricter than most people expect, and the racks fill early. If the purchase is tea, the Yunnan tea guide explains why the original cotton wrapper matters at customs, and Suzhou silk bargaining covers the other thing people end up posting home.
If the porcelain in that opening sentence is the reason you are here, Jingdezhen's markets run on a weekly clock — and most stallholders will pack and ship for you, which is usually better than doing it yourself.
