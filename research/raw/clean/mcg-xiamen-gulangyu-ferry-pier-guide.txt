The Gulangyu Pier You Can See Is Not the One You Can Use

The ferry pier you can see from central Xiamen is not the one you are allowed to use. Lundu Pier, five minutes from Zhongshan Road, is a residents' pier during the day. Turn up there with a foreign passport before half past five and you will be sent across the city.
Tourists sail to Gulangyu from the Xiamen International Cruise Center pier at Dongdu instead, which is a twenty-minute taxi from the centre. Book the crossing before you book anything else about the day.

The practical consequence is that a Gulangyu day starts with a cross-city taxi, not a stroll to the waterfront. Build forty minutes into the front of the day that no guide mentions, and book the sailing time in advance — the crossings are capacity-controlled and the island itself has a daily visitor cap.
The night route is the pleasant discovery in this. After 17:50 you sail from the pier in the middle of town, the crossing is short, and Gulangyu after the day-trippers have gone is a different island.

Gulangyu is a walking island
No cars, no bicycles, no scooters. The island is about two square kilometres of 19th and early 20th-century consular and merchant architecture — thirteen countries had consulates here — and it is a UNESCO site on the strength of that mix rather than any single building.
Two hours covers the main lanes. A full day lets you get lost in the back streets, which is the better version. The individual paid attractions inside are optional and mostly skippable; the island itself is the thing.
This is where you can actually eat Fujian food
Fujian cooking is one of the Eight Great Cuisines and one of the three that a normal China itinerary never reaches, because it needs you to be in Fujian. The regional cuisine guide makes that point in the abstract; Xiamen is where it stops being abstract.
What that means on a plate: broths and stocks rather than stir-fry heat, heavy use of mushrooms and seafood, and a sweetness that is closer to Jiangsu than to anything in Guangdong. Locally, oyster omelette (海蛎煎), satay noodles (沙茶面) which came back with returning migrants from Southeast Asia, and peanut soup, which is a dessert and is better than the description.
The rest of the city, and Quanzhou
Nanputuo Temple sits at the foot of Wulao Peak beside Xiamen University and is the city's working Buddhist temple rather than a museum of one. The university campus behind it is one of the most attractive in China and admits visitors with booking.
Zengcuo'an on the south coast is a fishing village absorbed into the city and now a dense grid of guest houses and food stalls. It is touristy and enjoyable, and it is where to stay if you want a beach rather than a high-rise.
The stronger argument for coming to Fujian at all is an hour and a half up the line. Quanzhou was the port Marco Polo called Zayton and the eastern end of the maritime Silk Road, and it receives almost no foreign visitors. If you have three days in Fujian, give one of them to Quanzhou.
Getting there
Xiamen is on the coastal high-speed line: about four and a half hours from Shanghai, three from Guangzhou, an hour and a half from Quanzhou. Booking on 12306 with a passport avoids the OTA fee, and the refund rules make booking early free to change your mind about.
Fujian is on the 240-hour visa-free transit list, so eligible passports can come without applying for anything provided the route continues to a third country — the transit rules cover what qualifies.
FAQ
Which pier do tourists use for Gulangyu?
Before 17:30, the Xiamen International Cruise Center Xiamen-Gulangyu Pier at Dongdu, about twenty minutes by taxi from the centre. Lundu Pier in the middle of town is a residents' pier by day and only opens visitor sailings from 17:50.
How much is the Gulangyu ferry?
¥35 for a standard crossing, ¥50 for deluxe seating and ¥158 for the island-circling route. First sailings from the Cruise Center are at 07:10, and crossings are capacity-controlled, so book ahead.
How long do I need on Gulangyu?
Two hours covers the main lanes and a full day is better, because the back streets are the point. There are no cars, bicycles or scooters on the island, so everything is on foot.
Is Xiamen worth it for the food?
It is one of the few places to eat Fujian cuisine properly, which most China itineraries never reach. Broths rather than stir-fry heat, and locally oyster omelette, satay noodles and peanut soup.
