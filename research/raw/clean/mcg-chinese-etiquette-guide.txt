The etiquette chapter in most China guidebooks was written in 1998 and nobody bothered to update it. You'll read that you must refuse tea three times before accepting, bow when greeting elders, and never speak until spoken to. Then you'll land in Shanghai, walk into a cafe, and watch a 25-year-old marketing exec in AirPods order a flat white with none of that happening. The gap between tourist-book China and actual 2026 China is wider than most people expect.
Here's the thing: real Chinese etiquette still exists, and breaking the rules that matter can genuinely embarrass your host or cost you a business deal. But the rules are fewer than you think, and they're less theatrical. This guide is the honest version. What actually matters at a dinner in Chengdu. How to hand over a business card in Guangzhou. What to do when your uncle-in-law keeps refilling your baijiu glass at a Beijing wedding. And which "rules" you can safely ignore.

The context: urban China versus traditional China
Modern mainland China is not one etiquette system. A tier-one city like Shanghai, Beijing, or Shenzhen operates on mostly Western-aligned norms with Chinese tradition layered on top at family meals and business events. A tier-three city or a village in Yunnan still observes customs that would feel formal and old-fashioned to a Shanghainese professional. Hong Kong has its own British-influenced blend. Taiwan preserves older mainland etiquette that's faded in the mainland since the 1980s.
For a tourist, this means: be more formal the further you get from the east coast cities, and always err on the side of politeness. Urban professionals will forgive small errors easily. Older rural hosts will be genuinely touched if you observe traditional forms even imperfectly.
The rule of thumb
Your host sets the tone. In a casual Shanghai coffee meeting, follow your host's relaxed lead. At a formal banquet in Xi'an, wait to be told where to sit, let your host pour first, and raise your glass to elders before drinking. When in doubt, mirror the behavior of the most senior Chinese person in the room.
Dining etiquette: the rules that still matter in 2026
Meals are where Chinese etiquette concentrates. A business meal, a family dinner, a wedding banquet — these are the settings where getting it wrong gets noticed, and getting it right earns real goodwill.
Chopstick rules that are non-negotiable
Never stick chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice. This mimics incense sticks at a funeral altar and is deeply inauspicious. Lay them across the bowl rim or on a chopstick rest.

Never tap chopsticks on the bowl. Beggars traditionally did this. It reads as crude.

Never point with chopsticks. Rude in the same way pointing with a fork is in Europe, but more so.

Don't pass food chopstick-to-chopstick. Funeral gesture (bones are transferred this way in cremation rituals).

Use serving chopsticks or the back end of your chopsticks when taking food from shared plates, especially in formal settings.

The seating hierarchy
At a round table banquet, the seat facing the door is the seat of honor — traditionally reserved for the most senior guest. Hosts sit opposite, with their back to the door. Younger or junior people sit on the sides. If you're a guest, wait until someone gestures for you to sit before choosing a chair. If you're clearly the honored guest, the host will direct you to the door-facing seat. Don't argue.
Ordering and sharing
Chinese meals are communal. The host orders for the table and dishes are shared from the center. Don't order your own individual plate at a shared meal — it breaks the social rhythm. If you have a dietary restriction, mention it before ordering begins so the host can accommodate.
Toasting: the gan bei ritual
Gan bei (干杯) literally means "dry the glass" — the equivalent of "bottoms up." At a formal meal, the host will propose the first toast, and everyone drinks together. You should toast back at some point during the meal, addressing the host or a senior figure with a short thank-you. When clinking glasses, hold your glass slightly lower than the person you're toasting as a sign of respect. This matters especially with elders and superiors.
You are not obligated to drain your glass unless someone explicitly says "gan bei." If the toast is general, a small sip is fine. If you don't drink alcohol, substitute tea or soft drinks — nobody in urban China will press you anymore, though at rural weddings or village banquets the pressure can still be real. "I'm on medication" is a universally accepted excuse.
Tipping: still not a thing
Tipping is not expected and is often refused. Restaurants, taxis, hotels, tour guides — none of them expect a tip. Some international hotels and tourist-oriented restaurants in Shanghai and Beijing are starting to accept tips, but it remains the exception. If you leave change on the table, the server will probably chase you down the street to return it. The only gray area is high-end tour guides and private drivers, where a ¥50 to ¥100 tip per day is appreciated but not required.
Paying the bill
Whoever invited pays. Don't insist on splitting — it's considered graceless. If you invited, pay without drama. If you were invited, a polite attempt to pay is acceptable, but accept graciously when your host insists. You can reciprocate at the next meal. Splitting the check ("going Dutch," or AA zhi 制) is common among young urban friends but almost never happens at business or formal meals.
Greetings, introductions, and body language
How to greet
A light handshake is standard in business and first-time social meetings across China. Bowing is Japanese and Korean, not Chinese — don't do it. Hugging is rare outside close friends. Kissing on the cheek is almost unheard of except among young urbanites with Western friends. A nod with a small smile works for casual encounters.
The common Chinese greeting is "ni hao" (你好), literally "you good" — hello. For a slightly more formal tone, "nin hao" (您好) uses the respectful form of "you." Add the person's title: Mr. Li is Li xiansheng (李先生), Ms. Wang is Wang nushi (王女士). In business, titles matter more than first names — Director Zhang, Manager Chen, Professor Liu.
Business card etiquette
This is one rule that still matters. Business cards in China are exchanged with both hands, Chinese side facing the recipient, with a slight nod. Receive the card with both hands, look at it for a moment, and place it on the table in front of you during the meeting — never shove it in your pocket or write on it. Handing or receiving a card one-handed reads as dismissive. Bring a stack of your own cards to any business meeting; running out is awkward.
Personal space and voice
Chinese cities are crowded, and personal space is tighter than in North America or northern Europe. Don't flinch when someone stands closer than you'd expect in a queue or on the subway. Voice volume varies by region: Cantonese speakers and northerners can be loud at meals; Shanghainese tend to be quieter. Follow the room.
Gift giving: the still-relevant rules
When to bring a gift
Bring a small gift when invited to someone's home for a meal, when visiting a business partner in a new city, or when meeting elders for the first time. Good gifts: tea, quality chocolate, a regional specialty from your home country, nice pens, a coffee table book about your city. Avoid: clocks (sending a clock, song zhong 送钟, sounds like "seeing someone off at death"), sharp objects (cutting the relationship), white or yellow flowers (funeral colors), and the number four in any quantity (sounds like "death").
How to give
Present gifts with both hands, Chinese side or nice side facing the recipient. The recipient may refuse once or twice out of politeness before accepting — this is dying out in urban China but still common with older hosts. Don't expect the gift to be opened in front of you, though many modern urban Chinese will do so. Red packaging is lucky, white is funereal.
Receiving gifts
Same rules in reverse. Two hands, a thank you, and no immediate opening unless the giver explicitly says "open it now."
Temple and religious site etiquette
Chinese temples (Buddhist, Taoist, Confucian) and mosques are active places of worship, not tourist props. The basic rules:
Dress modestly. Shoulders and knees covered. No tank tops or short shorts.

Remove hats inside halls. Sunglasses off.

Don't point at Buddha statues or deities. Open palm gesture instead.

Walk clockwise around stupas and main halls in Buddhist temples.

Photography rules vary. Exterior photos are usually fine. Interior photos of statues and altars are often prohibited — look for "no photo" signs or ask a monk.

Don't touch statues, offerings, or ritual objects.

Keep your voice down. Worshippers are praying. It's not a museum.

Incense is for worship, not play. If you light incense at an offering hall, do it respectfully. Three sticks is the traditional count.

Mosques in Xi'an, Urumqi, Kashgar, and elsewhere follow standard Islamic etiquette — non-Muslims may be restricted from prayer halls, and women should cover their hair at some sites.
Daily life: queuing, smoking, photos, volume
Queuing
Queuing in China has genuinely improved since the 2008 Olympics. In tier-one cities, you'll find orderly lines at subway gates, coffee shops, and popular restaurants. In tier-three cities and at train stations, the queue can still dissolve into a crush. Hold your ground politely, don't let people cut in front if it's a formal line, and accept some jostling in informal settings.
Smoking
Still common among older men, banned indoors in most restaurants and all public transit. Smoking in a hotel room will get you a cleaning fee. Secondhand smoke in older local restaurants is a reality.
Photographing strangers
Ask first, especially with older people, ethnic minorities in Yunnan or Xinjiang, and monks. "Keyi pai zhao ma?" (可以拍照吗 — can I take a photo?) with a smile goes a long way. Many rural elders will refuse, and that's their right. Tourist minorities in Yunnan sometimes charge a small posing fee — pay it or move on.
Voice volume in public
Loud phone conversations, FaceTiming without headphones, and video-watching on subway speakers are more tolerated in China than in Western Europe. You don't need to match the volume, but don't shush strangers — it won't work.

Business meeting protocol in 2026
Chinese business meetings still run more formally than Silicon Valley, less formally than Tokyo. Arrive on time (early is better), dress business formal for first meetings, exchange cards with two hands, and wait to be shown your seat. Small talk — weather, your flight, impressions of the city — precedes business discussion. Don't dive straight into the deal. Expect a meal invitation at some point; the real relationship work happens there.
What's faded and what hasn't
Faded: elaborate tea ceremonies at every meeting, gift-giving on first contact, extensive polite refusals. Not faded: the expectation that senior people speak first, the importance of the seating order, and the role of a banquet in closing a deal.
FAQ
Do you tip in China in 2026?
No. Tipping is not expected at restaurants, taxis, or hotels anywhere in mainland China in 2026. The one soft exception is high-end private tour guides and drivers, where ¥50 to ¥100 per day is appreciated. At regular meals, servers will often chase you down to return "forgotten" change.
What are the most important chopstick rules?
Three rules: never stick chopsticks upright in rice (mimics funeral incense), never pass food chopstick-to-chopstick (funeral gesture), and never point or tap with chopsticks (rude). Use serving chopsticks or the back end of your chopsticks at formal meals when taking from shared plates.
Is it rude to split the bill in China?
At a formal meal, a family gathering, or a business dinner, yes — the host is expected to pay. Splitting the bill (called AA zhi) is normal among young urban friends at casual meals, but insisting on splitting at a meal where you were clearly invited will embarrass your host. Reciprocate by paying at the next outing.
How should I greet people in China?
A light handshake with a nod and "ni hao" works in most settings. In business, use titles (Manager Li, Director Wang) rather than first names. Don't bow — that's Japanese and Korean etiquette. Hugging and kissing on the cheek are rare and reserved for close friends in urban environments.
What gifts should I bring when invited to a Chinese home?
Good gifts: quality tea, nice chocolate, a regional specialty from your home country, a coffee table book about your city, wine or liquor. Avoid: clocks (funeral association), sharp objects (cuts the relationship), white or yellow flowers (funeral colors), and anything in groups of four. Present the gift with two hands.
Most etiquette in practice is table etiquette, and the mechanics matter more than the rituals: meals are shared, the unit is the table rather than the person, and ordering four spicy dishes in a row is the mistake everyone makes once.
