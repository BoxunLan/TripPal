Taking a Teenager to China: The Rules That Change Under 18

Everything you have read about Beijing, Xi'an and Chengdu still applies with a fifteen-year-old along. What changes is a short list of rules that attach to the age, and they are the things families find out at the worst possible moment.
Start with the one that breaks a day: a traveller under 18 cannot complete real-name verification on Alipay. No verification means no working account, which means your teenager cannot pay for anything themselves. Everything has to route through a parent's phone.

Two phones, two jobs
Split the apps rather than duplicating them. The parent's phone is the wallet; the teenager's is the map and the camera.
Parent: Alipay with Tour Pass and a linked Visa or Mastercard, WeChat Pay as backup with real-name verification done, Trip.com for rail and hotels, 12306 for last-minute changes.

Teen: Amap set to English — genuinely better than Apple Maps for walking a hutong. Didi, which runs in English and settles through the parent's Alipay by QR. And Xiaohongshu, which is the best travel-content app in China for exactly this age group.

Set up Alipay and WeChat Pay before you fly. Both take half an hour and neither is fun to do at an airport.
The Instagram problem
Instagram, Snapchat, WhatsApp, Facebook and YouTube are all blocked on a mainland SIM. For a teenager who posts daily, this is not a minor inconvenience.
The fix that actually works is a roaming eSIM installed before departure — Airalo, Nomad or Holafly, routing through Hong Kong or Singapore, which sidesteps the firewall because the traffic never touches the Chinese internet. A pre-installed VPN is the backup, and enforcement has tightened enough in 2026 that it should not be the only plan. International chain hotels usually run a guest network that reaches blocked services ( which eSIM to buy ).
Worth saying to the teenager before the trip: Xiaohongshu does the job Instagram does, and does it better for travel. Some of them take to it immediately and stop caring about the firewall.
A route that works in seven days
Beijing and Shanghai, heavy on the icons, light on travel days.
Days 1–2: Beijing. Forbidden City in the morning, Jingshan for the view over it, Qianmen in the evening. First lesson in scanning a QR code to pay for something.

Day 3: Mutianyu. Cable car up, walk four to six watchtowers, toboggan down — 1,580 m of track and, reliably, the thing they talk about afterwards. About ¥220 all in.

Day 4: Temple of Heaven, then 798 Art Zone, which photographs well and is the one contemporary stop teenagers actually want.

Days 5–7: Shanghai by high-speed rail, 4h28m, ¥553 second class. The Bund, the French Concession, and Disney if it is in the budget.

Ten days adds Xi'an and Chengdu: Beijing to Xi'an 4h18m ¥515, Xi'an to Chengdu about 3h30m ¥263, Chengdu to Shanghai 10–11 hours ¥874. On that last leg first class is worth the extra — wider seats and a quieter carriage for eleven hours ( the Beijing guide and the Xi'an guide have each city's costs).
The two stops that land hardest with this age group are the panda base in Chengdu — be at the gate at 07:30, because they sleep through the afternoon, ¥55 — and the Terracotta Warriors, ¥120 plus ¥45 for the shuttle, where an hour on site is genuinely enough.
Food, and the spice conversation
The pattern is consistent: cautious on day one, converted by soup dumplings, adventurous by day four. Xiaolongbao in Shanghai, Peking duck as a dinner event, hand-pulled Lanzhou beef noodles at ¥20–35, jianbing from a morning cart at ¥8–12.
Go easy on Sichuan on the first day. Ask for 微辣 (wēi là, slightly spicy) rather than 中辣 or 特辣 until you know how they handle it ( the Chengdu street food walk has the phrases).
Safety, honestly
China is one of the safer places to travel with a teenager — violent crime against visitors is rare and public transport is heavily monitored. The real risks are ordinary: pickpocketing at tourist sites, unlicensed taxis, and getting separated in a crowd with no working phone payment.
Save these on the teenager's phone: police 110, ambulance 120, fire 119, and the English-language tourist hotline 12301.
Tap water is not drinkable anywhere in mainland China. Pharmacies (药店) sell over-the-counter medicine without a prescription, and the international clinics in Beijing and Shanghai are the ones to use if something needs more than that.

FAQ
Can a teenager have their own Alipay in China?
No. Real-name verification requires the account holder to be 18 or over, so an under-18 cannot open a usable Alipay or link a card. All payments go through a parent's phone.
Can a 16-year-old travel to China alone?
Not practically. Hotels will not check in unaccompanied minors, airlines restrict solo travel by age, and payment apps are closed to under-18s. The workable versions are a parent travelling along, a licensed youth operator, or a formal exchange programme with a verified host family.
Do teenagers get discounted attraction tickets?
Not on a foreign passport. Most mainland sites give no child or student discount to overseas visitors — budget the adult price, which at the Forbidden City is ¥60.
Will Instagram work for my teenager in China?
Not on a mainland SIM. A roaming eSIM installed before departure, routing through Hong Kong or Singapore, is the reliable fix; a pre-installed VPN is a backup rather than a plan.
When is the best time to take a teenager to China?
April, May, September and October give the best weather across Beijing, Xi'an, Chengdu and Shanghai. July and August are hot and humid, and school-holiday crowds are at their worst.
