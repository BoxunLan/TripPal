Does Facebook (and Messenger) Work in China?

Jenny Wang · Local Expert, Beijing Native · Updated September 14, 2026

No. Facebook and Facebook Messenger do not work in mainland China. Both have been blocked by the Great Firewall since July 2009, making it the longest-standing ban of any major Western platform. To use them on your trip you need a roaming eSIM or a VPN, and you must set it up before you land.
The Short Answer: Facebook Has Been Blocked Since 2009
No, Facebook does not work in mainland China, and it hasn't for a long time. Open the app or visit facebook.com on a Chinese network and the page simply times out. No error explanation, no login screen, just an endless spinner. The same is true the moment you try to refresh your feed or send a message.
Facebook Messenger is blocked too. People assume messaging apps slip through where the main site is censored, but Messenger runs on Meta's infrastructure and is filtered at the same network level. You won't be able to send or receive messages, make calls, or even see whether your texts delivered. If staying reachable through Messenger matters to you, treat it as offline until you fix your connection.
This is not a temporary glitch or a regional hiccup. Facebook was first blocked in July 2009, in the days after the Ürümqi riots in Xinjiang, when authorities said the platform was being used to coordinate unrest. It has stayed blocked every day since. That makes Facebook the longest-standing ban of any major Western platform in China, older than the blocks on Instagram, WhatsApp, and YouTube that came later.

What Else Is Blocked (It's Not Just Facebook)
If Facebook is part of your daily routine, the rest of your usual toolkit is probably affected too. The Great Firewall blocks the entire Meta family, so Instagram and WhatsApp are out alongside Facebook and Messenger. Google is gone in full, which means Gmail, Google Search, Google Drive, Google Maps, and YouTube all fail to load. X (formerly Twitter) is blocked as well.
The block is applied at the network gateway, not to your individual account, so there is nothing wrong with your phone or your login. Every connection leaving mainland China passes through a handful of checkpoints, and traffic to these services is filtered there before it ever reaches you. That is why the block is total and consistent: it doesn't matter whether you're on mobile data, hotel Wi-Fi, or airport Wi-Fi.
The practical takeaway is that fixing Facebook means fixing all of it at once. Both solutions below restore your whole blocked toolkit in one move, not just one app, so you don't need a separate workaround for each platform.
The Two Ways Back Online: Roaming eSIM vs VPN
There are two realistic ways to use Facebook and Messenger in China, and in 2026 they are not equally reliable.
A VPN can still work. It routes your traffic through an encrypted tunnel to a server abroad, which lets you reach blocked sites. But VPNs have become noticeably less dependable since the April 2026 crackdown reported by Radio Free Asia, when the three Chinese carriers began blocking VPN-signature IP addresses on detection with no grace period. Long-term users report that many services now drop out without warning, work one hour and fail the next, and that you often need two or three apps installed as backups. If you go this route, set up more than one VPN before you travel and don't rely on any single brand.
A roaming eSIM is the more reliable fix in 2026, and it works in a fundamentally different way. Instead of fighting the firewall, it sidesteps it. A roaming eSIM connects to a Chinese network for signal but routes your mobile data out through a foreign network, so your traffic exits China and never passes through the Great Firewall's filters. There is no VPN to install, no app to launch, and nothing for the carriers to detect and block. You turn on data roaming and Facebook, Messenger, Instagram, and Google all just work. For most travelers this is the path of least resistance, and it's why we point readers to a roaming eSIM as the default recommendation. Our full comparison of the best eSIM for China breaks down the providers worth considering.
Set It Up Before You Land (This Is the Step People Miss)
Here is the mistake that strands people at the arrivals hall: they plan to download a VPN or buy an eSIM after they get to China. You can't. The app stores serve censored listings inside China, VPN provider websites are themselves blocked, and the pages you'd need to complete a purchase won't load. By the time you've landed, the tools that would get you online are exactly the tools you can no longer reach.
Everything has to be in place before you cross the border. Install and test your VPN apps while you're still on a normal connection, and sign in so the apps are ready to connect. If you're going the eSIM route, buy and install the eSIM profile in advance over your home Wi-Fi, then leave it switched off until you land. A roaming eSIM activates the moment you arrive and enable data roaming, with no setup needed on Chinese soil.
Build in a little buffer. Do this a day or two before you fly, not at the gate, so you have time to confirm everything connects. If a VPN won't establish a tunnel or an eSIM profile won't install, you want to discover that at home where you can still fix it.
The Wi-Fi Trap That Catches eSIM Users
This one surprises almost everyone, so it's worth being clear. A roaming eSIM only bypasses the Great Firewall on its own mobile data. The instant you connect to hotel Wi-Fi, café Wi-Fi, or any local network, your traffic routes back through Chinese infrastructure and the firewall applies again, even with the eSIM sitting in your phone.
In practice this means Facebook loads fine while you're walking around on eSIM data, then dies the moment you sit down in your hotel and your phone auto-joins the lobby Wi-Fi. They are two separate connections with two completely different routes out of the country, and only one of them avoids the censorship.
The fix is simple once you know it: keep your eSIM data on, and either turn Wi-Fi off or don't trust it for blocked apps. If you want Facebook and Messenger to keep working on local Wi-Fi, that's the scenario where a VPN earns its place, because the VPN tunnels your traffic out regardless of which network you're physically connected to. Many seasoned travelers carry both for exactly this reason. You can see which VPN services hold up best in our guide to the best VPN for China .
Device Specifics and a Note on Maps
For the eSIM route, your phone needs to be both eSIM-capable and carrier-unlocked. Most iPhones from the XS onward and recent Google Pixel and Samsung Galaxy models support eSIM, but a handful of regional variants do not, so confirm yours before you buy. The setup itself is just scanning a QR code or tapping an install link over Wi-Fi at home.
While you're sorting out connectivity, plan for navigation too, because Google Maps is blocked alongside Facebook. Here's a useful quirk: if your iPhone's region is set to anything other than China, Apple Maps automatically switches to Amap's licensed data inside the country and works with no VPN at all. The local apps Amap and Baidu Maps also work natively and are excellent. Avoid leaning on Google Maps even through a VPN , since it renders China on offset coordinates that can place you hundreds of meters from where you actually are.
Staying in Touch Without Facebook
Once you accept that Facebook and Messenger will be unreliable, the smoother move is to lean on what actually works in China. WeChat is the country's all-in-one app for messaging, voice and video calls, and payments, and crucially it works globally. Install it before you travel and have family back home install it too, and you have a stable channel that needs no VPN and no eSIM workaround to function.
For following local news, events, and recommendations during your trip, Weibo fills the role Facebook and X play elsewhere, and Xiaohongshu (Little Red Book) is the go-to for travel tips, restaurant reviews, and lifestyle content. None of these require any special setup. They are simply the platforms that run on the open Chinese internet.
Our honest advice for a smooth trip: set up a roaming eSIM so your blocked apps work on the go, install WeChat as your reliable everyday lifeline, and treat a VPN as a backup for Wi-Fi situations rather than your main plan. That combination keeps you connected without betting your whole trip on a single tool that the firewall might knock offline.
FAQ
Is Facebook permanently banned in China?
There is no fixed end date, but the block has held continuously since July 2009, making it the longest-running ban of any major Western platform. There is no sign of it lifting, so plan your trip assuming Facebook and Messenger will not work on a normal Chinese connection.
Does Facebook Messenger work in China if Facebook itself is blocked?
No. Messenger runs on Meta's infrastructure and is filtered by the Great Firewall at the same network level as Facebook. You won't be able to send messages, make calls, or see delivery status without a roaming eSIM or VPN.
Can I use a free VPN to access Facebook in China?
Free VPNs are the least reliable option and most are detected and blocked quickly, especially after the April 2026 crackdown when carriers began blocking VPN-signature IPs on sight. If you want dependable access, a roaming eSIM avoids the VPN cat-and-mouse entirely by routing your data out through a foreign network.
Why does Facebook work on my eSIM data but not on hotel Wi-Fi?
Because they are two different routes out of China. A roaming eSIM sends your mobile data through a foreign network that the firewall doesn't filter, but hotel and café Wi-Fi route through Chinese infrastructure where the block still applies. Keep your eSIM data on, or use a VPN when you need Facebook on local Wi-Fi.
Do I need to set up a VPN or eSIM before arriving in China?
Yes, this is essential. App stores serve censored listings inside China and VPN websites are themselves blocked, so you cannot download or buy these tools after you land. Install and test everything over your home Wi-Fi a day or two before you fly.
What do locals use instead of Facebook?
WeChat is the dominant all-in-one app for messaging, calls, and payments, and it works worldwide so family at home can use it too. Weibo and Xiaohongshu cover the social feed and recommendations roles, and all of them run on the open Chinese internet with no workaround needed.
Worth knowing before you decide the app matters: the Chinese replacements are not a consolation prize, and you will end up using WeChat anyway because hotels and drivers communicate through it. Which Chinese apps are actually worth installing covers the set, and linking a foreign card to WeChat Pay takes about fifteen minutes at home and is close to compulsory once you land.
