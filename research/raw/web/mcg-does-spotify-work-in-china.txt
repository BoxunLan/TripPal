Does Spotify Work in China? The 2026 Answer + Offline Download Strategy

Jenny Wang · Local Expert, Beijing Native · Updated September 14, 2026

Spotify does not stream in mainland China — the app cannot reach its servers on a Chinese SIM or hotel WiFi, because the service never launched there and the Great Firewall blocks it. But here is the loophole that saves your trip: playlists you download with Spotify Premium before arrival play perfectly offline, in airplane mode or on any blocked network, because the files live on your phone. Premium allows up to 10,000 downloaded songs per device on up to five devices, and you only need to let the app touch the internet once every 30 days to keep them active. To stream normally inside China, use an international roaming eSIM (about ¥70-140 / $10-20 per week) that routes your data outside the firewall, or a VPN with strong obfuscation installed before you land. Download first, though — offline playback is the one method that never fails.
The short answer: no streaming, yes offline
Let's separate the two things people actually mean when they ask this question.
Can Spotify connect and stream in mainland China? No. Spotify has never launched in the Chinese market — there is no mainland catalog, no local licensing, no yuan pricing — and on top of that, the Great Firewall blocks access to Spotify's servers. Open the app fresh on a Chinese network and it will spin, fail to load your home screen, and eventually tell you it can't connect. This puts Spotify in the same bucket as most Western platforms; the full list of what's blocked in China is worth scanning before any trip.
Can Spotify play music in China? Absolutely — if the music is already on your phone. Downloaded playlists are stored locally and play without any network at all. Last October, a client from Denver did a twelve-day loop through Beijing, Xi'an, and Chengdu with his entire running library downloaded, and Spotify worked flawlessly the whole trip precisely because it never needed to connect. He streamed nothing and missed nothing.
That distinction is the whole game. Treat Spotify in China the way you'd treat it on a long-haul flight: an offline music player that you loaded up beforehand.

The offline loophole: how Premium downloads behave in China
The mechanics matter here, because they decide what you do before you fly.
Downloads are real files on your device. When you download a playlist, album, or podcast with Premium, Spotify stores encrypted audio files locally. Playback checks nothing over the network. Airplane mode, Chinese SIM, blocked WiFi — none of it matters. This is the same reason downloads work on the subway or over the Pacific.
The limits are generous. Premium allows up to 10,000 downloaded songs per device, across up to five devices. Nobody is running out of room on a two-week trip; your constraint is phone storage, not Spotify's cap.
The 30-day rule is the one real catch. Spotify requires the app to go online at least once every 30 days to verify your subscription. Stay offline longer than that and your downloads stop playing until the app reconnects. For a typical one- or two-week China trip, you will never hit it. For a month-plus stay, one brief session on a roaming eSIM or VPN resets the clock — I tell long-stay clients to just open Spotify whenever their eSIM data is on, and the check-in happens on its own.
Music downloads require Premium. On the free tier you can download podcasts, but not songs or albums. If there was ever a month to pay for Premium (about ¥70 / $11.99 for an individual plan in the US), it is the month you travel to China.
Enable Offline Mode on landing. In settings, switch on Offline Mode when you arrive. It stops the app from endlessly hunting for servers on a blocked network, which otherwise drains battery and can make the app feel broken even though your downloads are fine.
Your pre-flight download plan
Here's the routine I give clients, ideally done on home WiFi the night before you fly:
Check your storage first. At the default high quality, figure very roughly a gigabyte per couple hundred songs. A 1,000-song library fits comfortably on most phones, but check before you start, not at 2 a.m.

Download by playlist, not by song. Hit download on your daily-driver playlists, a couple of albums you've been meaning to sit with, and one long "everything I like" mix. Watch for the green arrow that confirms each one finished.

Add podcasts and audiobooks. High-speed rail legs like Beijing to Xi'an (about 4.5 hours) are prime listening time. Download more episodes than you think you need.

Set download quality deliberately. "Very High" sounds better and eats more space; "Normal" roughly doubles what fits. For earbuds on a noisy metro, most people cannot tell the difference.

Do not log out, and do not reinstall. Logging out deletes your downloads, and reinstalling the app inside China means re-downloading everything through a blocked or throttled connection. Leave the app alone once it's loaded.

Test it. Put your phone in airplane mode at home and play three different playlists. If they play in your kitchen with no network, they will play in Chengdu.

Streaming inside China: the eSIM and VPN fixes
Downloads cover listening, but maybe you want the full live app — new releases, playlist edits syncing, that one song a friend texts you. Two fixes work in 2026, and they are the same two that fix every blocked app.
A roaming eSIM is the low-effort option. A travel eSIM from an international provider tunnels your mobile data back through its home carrier in Hong Kong or Singapore before it reaches the internet, so it exits outside the firewall. Spotify streams normally, no VPN involved, nothing to configure. Expect around ¥70-140 / $10-20 per week of data; our China eSIM comparison covers current picks. Install it before you fly, and remember it only protects its own mobile data — on hotel WiFi you are back behind the firewall.
A VPN covers WiFi and laptops. With a VPN connected, Spotify behaves as if you were in your home country. The caveat in 2026: China's April crackdown on VPN traffic signatures broke many services, so choose from VPNs verified to still work in China and install before departure — VPN sites are blocked from inside the country.
One habit worth building: streaming over an eSIM burns data quickly. A week of commute-length listening can chew through a couple of gigabytes on its own. Downloads over hotel WiFi with the VPN on, streaming saved for moments that need it — that mix keeps both your data plan and your battery happy.
Local alternatives: QQ Music and NetEase Cloud Music
Worth knowing about, with honest expectations.
China has a rich domestic streaming scene, dominated by QQ Music (Tencent) and NetEase Cloud Music. Both offer free ad-supported tiers, both have enormous Chinese catalogs, and both carry a meaningful chunk of Western music through licensing deals — Tencent has held deals with all three Western majors at various points. Plenty of foreign residents use them daily.
For a short-term visitor, the friction is real, though. The apps are almost entirely in Chinese, sign-up works best with a Chinese phone number, and Western catalog coverage is patchy: some artists are fully there, others missing entire albums, depending on current licensing. Payment for premium tiers generally expects Alipay or WeChat Pay — which, to be fair, foreigners can now set up with an international card .
My honest verdict: as a two-week traveler, don't bother replacing your library. Download your Spotify collection before you fly and enjoy it offline. Try QQ Music or NetEase as a cultural side quest if you're curious — discovering Chinese indie and C-pop through NetEase's playlists is genuinely fun — but as an experiment, not a dependency.
If you're an Apple ecosystem person, one more option: Apple Music operates legally in mainland China, and travelers consistently report that foreign Apple Music accounts keep streaming there without a VPN, though catalogs differ by region. It is one of the few Western entertainment services with a sanctioned mainland presence.
Frequently asked questions
Will my downloaded playlists stop working during a long China trip?
Only if Spotify stays fully offline for more than 30 days — Premium requires an occasional online check-in to verify your subscription. On a trip under a month, you'll never notice the rule. On a longer stay, opening the app once while on a roaming eSIM or VPN resets the 30-day clock and your downloads carry on.
Does Spotify Free work in China?
Effectively no. The free tier cannot download music, and streaming is blocked on Chinese networks, which removes both ways Free-tier users listen. Free accounts can download podcasts for offline playback, but for music you need Premium plus pre-trip downloads, or an eSIM/VPN to stream.
Does Spotify work in Hong Kong and Macau?
Yes, normally. Hong Kong and Macau sit outside the Great Firewall, and Spotify officially serves the Hong Kong market. Your app will stream, sync, and update there with no workarounds — a good moment to refresh downloads before heading to the mainland.
Can I just use Apple Music instead?
Largely yes. Apple Music has an official licensed service in mainland China, and foreign accounts generally continue to work on Chinese networks without a VPN — an unusual exception among Western services. Expect some regional catalog gaps, and download your key albums before you fly anyway, since offline files are the only guarantee that survives any network surprise.
What should I do with Spotify the moment I land?
Turn on Offline Mode in the app's settings. It stops Spotify from repeatedly trying to reach blocked servers, which saves battery and avoids the app hanging on a loading screen. Your downloads play instantly in Offline Mode, and you can toggle it off whenever you're on your eSIM data or VPN.
