The Eight Great Cuisines Is Not a Travel Plan

Sarah Liu · Cultural Guide, Food Explorer · Updated September 27, 2026

The Eight Great Cuisines (八大菜系) is a late-Qing classification of where cooking traditions came from. It is not a menu, and it is not a travel plan. Three of the eight you will almost certainly not eat on a normal trip, and two of the things you will eat most are not in the list at all.
So here is the framework, followed by the more useful question: given where you are actually going, which of these can you eat a good version of?
For a shortlist of what to eat on a first trip, with prices and ordering tips, see the best local food in China for tourists ; this page is about how the regions divide and where each is worth eating.

Which leaves the three you will most likely never eat: Shandong, Fujian and Anhui. Shandong survives mostly as technique inside Beijing banquet cooking rather than as a restaurant you would seek out. Fujian and Anhui need you to go to Fujian and Anhui. If a restaurant in Shanghai advertises 徽菜, it is not a reason to change your plans.

The four that are not in the eight
On a typical two-week route you will eat more of these than of Shandong, Fujian and Anhui combined.
Northwest and Xinjiang. Hand-pulled 拉条子, 大盘鸡, 抓饭, lamb skewers, clay-oven naan. Cumin-forward and unmistakable. Good in Xi'an, good in Beijing, unbeatable in Xinjiang itself .

Dongbei. Northeastern food: big cold-weather plates, pickled cabbage, dumplings, stewed everything. Portion sizes that defeat two people.

Yunnan. Mushrooms, rice noodles, goat cheese, southeast-Asian borrowings. Kunming, Dali, Lijiang.

Tibetan and Himalayan. Barley 糌粑 at ¥10–25, butter tea ¥5–20 a bowl, yak momos ¥15–30, yak yoghurt. Lhasa, Shigatse, Gannan.

Ordering without getting it wrong
The mechanics matter more than the vocabulary . Meals are shared, so the unit is the table, not the person.
Two people: three dishes plus a rice or noodle. Four people: five or six dishes, a soup and a staple.

Spread the flavours rather than stacking them — one cold starter, one meat, one vegetable, one tofu or fish, one staple. Four spicy dishes in a row is a mistake you make once.

Spice level is a real dial: 不辣 none, 微辣 mild, 中辣 medium, 特辣 extra. In Hunan, 不辣 is understood as a suggestion.

Ask for 招牌菜 (zhāo pái cài), the house speciality. Most kitchens have two or three they are proud of and will steer you to them.

Dianping lists the most-ordered dishes with photographs, which works even when the menu does not.

FAQ
Is Chinese food all spicy?
No. Sichuan and Hunan bring heat; Cantonese, Jiangsu, Zhejiang and Shandong are mild by design. You could eat for a month without a chilli if you chose your cities. Menus usually mark heat with a pepper icon.
What is the difference between Sichuan and Hunan?
Sichuan pairs chilli with Sichuan peppercorn, which numbs — that buzzing sensation is 麻, and it is a separate axis from heat. Hunan is closer to pure chilli, often fresh or fermented, with a sour edge from pickles. Hunan is louder; Sichuan is more layered.
Do people in China eat General Tso's chicken?
No. General Tso's, beef and broccoli, crab rangoon and orange chicken are twentieth-century inventions by Chinese immigrants cooking for Western palates. Fortune cookies are Japanese-American. None of it exists on a Chinese menu.
Is street food safe?
At a busy stall with high turnover, yes — the queue is the quality control. Skip empty stalls, pre-cut fruit sitting in the open, and anything that has been warming for hours. Watch it cooked where you can.
If I only get one city for food, which?
Chengdu, on density and price. Guangzhou is the more refined argument and Xi'an the more surprising one, but Chengdu puts more good food within walking distance for less money than either.
