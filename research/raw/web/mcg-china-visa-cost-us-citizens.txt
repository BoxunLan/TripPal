$140 . That's the current China visa fee for US citizens -- single entry, double entry, or the full 10-year multiple entry. Same price for all of them. That's a reduced rate, too (normally $185), valid through December 31, 2026. But $140 is just the consular fee. The real total? Depends on how you apply. Could be $140. Could be more, if you pay for express service or an agency.

This article is part of our complete guide to China visa requirements for Americans . If you're not sure whether you even need a visa, start there first.
How Much Does a China Visa Cost for US Citizens?
Short answer: $140 flat . Single entry, double entry, multiple entry including the 10-year -- all the same price for Americans. The only exception is the J1/J2 journalist visa at $171 .
Now here's what most sites won't tell you. That $140 is actually a discount. The standard rate is $185. China cut it from December 11, 2023, saying it wanted to make people-to-people exchanges easier, and has extended the cut twice -- first through 2025, now through December 31, 2026 .
Why do Americans pay so much? Reciprocity. China raised the fee for US citizens to $185 in May 2023 on that principle, and the US charges Chinese citizens $185 to apply for a visitor visa. Non-US citizens applying in the US pay far less: $23 for a single entry, up to $68 for multiple entries over 12 months or more. Americans pay $140 because that's the "discounted" version of matching the US fee.
The China visa application fee sounds steep compared to other nationalities, but for that $140 you can get a multiple-entry visa valid for up to 10 years. When you run the numbers per trip, it's actually a solid deal.

See the pattern? Americans pay one flat rate whatever the number of entries, and so do Canadians applying in Toronto, while other nationalities applying in the US pay by the number of entries. Fees vary a lot by nationality and by where you apply, and visa centers add their own service fees, so check the fee page of the embassy or visa center you'll use.
One big 2026 caveat for non-US readers: citizens of 50 countries, including the UK, Canada, Australia, Japan, and most of the EU, can skip the visa entirely for stays of up to 30 days under China's unilateral visa-free policy, which runs through December 31, 2026 for most of them. Americans are not on the visa-free list, which is exactly why the 10-year visa math in this guide matters most for US passport holders.

The Real Total Cost (What You'll Actually Pay)
OK here's where it gets interesting. Nobody talks about this part. The $140 consular fee is one line item. Depending on how you apply, you may also pay for express service, an agency, photos, and the trip to the visa office.

Don't pay an agency just because you're in a hurry. If you can hand in the application yourself, an agency adds its handling fee on top of the same $140 visa fee for the same form.
When each option makes sense:
Apply in person ($140-165) : Best value. You hand in your passport at the Chinese Embassy or consulate that covers your state : the embassy in Washington, D.C., or the consulates in New York, Chicago, San Francisco, and Los Angeles. There is no longer a consulate in Houston; its states now go to the embassy. No appointment is needed. Not possible for everyone, but if you can swing it, do it.

Visa agency ($140-165 plus the agency's fee) : Worth it if you can't get to the visa office yourself, because there's no mail-in option: you or an agent must hand in the passport. I think most people who live near a visa office don't need this.

Pro tip : If you live within driving distance of the visa office for your state, apply directly and skip the agency fee. The process is identical -- you're just cutting out the middleman. You pay when you pick up the visa, by Mastercard, Visa, money order, or cashier's check (no cash or personal checks).

What the 10-Year Visa Actually Gets You
This trips people up. A 10-year Chinese visa for US citizens doesn't mean you can stay in China for 10 years. Not even close.
Here's what it actually means:
Validity : Up to 10 years from the issue date (your window to enter China)

Entries : Unlimited while the visa is valid

Stay per visit : Set by the consular officer and printed on the visa as the "Duration of Each Stay", counted from the day you enter

Can you extend? Yes. Apply to the local exit-entry administration (part of the Public Security Bureau) at least seven days before your stay runs out. A tourist visa stay can be extended by up to 30 days, and the fee depends on your nationality.

So the 10-year multiple entry is really a "visit as many times as you want over 10 years, but leave before each stay runs out" visa. For most people planning trips to China, that's more than enough.
The overstay penalty is no joke. Under China's Exit and Entry Administration Law, overstaying gets you a warning or, in serious cases, a fine of ¥500 (about $75) a day, capped at ¥10,000 (about $1,480), or 5 to 15 days of detention. Don't test it.
What Happens When Your Passport Expires?
Your passport will almost certainly expire before your 10-year visa does. When that happens:
Bring both passports : If your name, gender, date of birth, and nationality haven't changed and your trip matches the visa type, the embassy says you don't need a new visa. Enter China with your new passport and the old one containing the valid visa.

Replace the visa in China : If you're already in China with a new passport, the local exit-entry administration can issue a replacement visa with the same type, validity, and stay. The fee depends on your nationality.

Option 1 is the easier route. Just make sure the old passport isn't damaged: if the visa page is torn or water-stained, apply for a new visa before you travel.
How to Apply (Simpler Than You Think)
Good news. Since January 2024, China dropped a bunch of requirements that used to make the tourist visa (L visa) application painful. You no longer need:
~~Round-trip flight tickets~~

~~Hotel bookings~~

~~Tour itinerary~~

~~Invitation letter~~

This is a big deal. They used to want your complete travel plan before they'd issue a visa. Now? Just these (officers can still ask for more, case by case):
What you actually need:
Passport : More than 6 months of remaining validity and at least 2 blank visa pages (upload the bio page and a blank visa page, then bring the original)

Application form : Completed online at consular.mfa.gov.cn/VISA , the system the embassy switched to on September 30, 2025

Photo : 48mm x 33mm, white background , taken within 6 months

Previous Chinese visas : Upload your latest Chinese visa, and bring the old passport if that visa is still valid

Proof of residence : A driver's license, utility bill, or bank bill showing you live in the consular district

Proof of legal US status : Only if you're not a US citizen (green card, visa, I-20, or I-94). Former Chinese citizens applying for the first time since naturalizing also upload their Chinese passport and naturalization certificate

That's the whole list. Fill out the online form ( some of the questions are weirdly specific about your employment history , but just answer honestly) and upload your documents. Once the status shows "Passport to be submitted", print the application page with the barcode and take it, with your passport, to the visa office for your state, or have an agent do it. There's no mail-in option. If an earlier application was refused, check the common rejection reasons and fixes before you apply again.
Processing times:
Standard: 4 business days

Express: 3 business days (+$25), for urgent travel with supporting documents

Timing: the online system only accepts applications for trips starting within the next 90 days, so don't apply too early. Processing time counts from the day you hand in your passport, and some applications take longer. Check our China travel guides for timing your trip around the best seasons.
Fingerprints? The embassy says applicants don't need to come in person to have fingerprints taken, though a consular officer can still ask you in for an interview. One less thing to deal with.

At $14/year for unlimited entries, the 10-year visa is probably the best deal in international travel. But if you're on the fence about whether you'll even like China, the visa-free route lets you try it for free.
FAQ
Do I Need EVUS to Use My 10-Year China Visa?
No. EVUS (Electronic Visa Update System) is for Chinese nationals traveling to the US on a 10-year B1/B2 visa. Americans traveling to China do not need EVUS, and there's no equivalent registration for a Chinese visa. You do fill in an arrival card when you enter China, and since November 20, 2025 you can do that online before you fly.
What If My Passport Expires Before My 10-Year Visa?
Carry both your old passport (with the valid visa) and your new passport when entering China, as long as your name, gender, date of birth, and nationality haven't changed. If they have, apply for a new visa. If you're already in China, the local exit-entry administration can replace the visa in your new passport for a fee.
Can First-Time Visitors Get the 10-Year Visa?
Yes. No prior China travel is required. Just know that the consular officer decides the validity, number of entries, and length of each stay, so a 10-year visa isn't guaranteed.
Will the $140 Reduced Rate Continue After 2026?
The Chinese embassy extended the reduced fee schedule through December 31, 2026 (announced in late December 2025), so the $140 rate is locked for all of 2026. The full price is $185, and there's no official word yet on 2027. If the 10-year visa makes sense for your travel plans, applying while the reduction is active saves $45.
How Much Is a Chinese Visa for Non-US Citizens?
It depends on your nationality and where you apply. Non-US citizens applying in the US pay $23 for a single entry, $34 for double, $45 for six-month multiple entry, and $68 for 12 months or more, though some nationalities pay different rates. Visa centers abroad add a service fee: in Toronto, a Canadian pays CAD 137.15 in total for regular service. And citizens of 50 countries, including the UK, Canada, and Australia, can visit for up to 30 days without a visa through December 31, 2026.
Is Express Processing Worth the Extra $25?
Depends on your timeline. Standard takes 4 business days, express cuts it to 3, and it's meant for urgent travel: you show supporting documents when you ask for it. If you planned ahead, save the $25. If your trip is in two weeks and you haven't started the application... yeah, ask for express. The visa you get is identical -- just faster.

So that's the full picture on the $140 10-year visa for China -- what it costs, what it gets you, and whether it's worth the money. For most Americans planning repeat trips, it's the best $140 you'll spend on travel prep.
Michael Chen is a Shanghai native and travel writer who has taken 200+ train journeys across China. He keeps a budget spreadsheet for everything -- including the exact cost of every visa he's ever applied for.
Before you pay anything, check whether you need to. The visa-free routes cover several separate schemes, and two of them work for independent trips on a US passport: 240-hour transit if your route continues to a third country, and Hainan's own 30-day policy , which needs no onward ticket at all. If the trip is mainland China for two weeks, neither works and the $140 is the answer — the step-by-step application walks through the form, and processing times tell you how early to start.
