China Visa-Free Countries List 2026: Full Rules, Stays, and Policies

China's borders are more open in 2026 than at any point in decades, but "visa-free" has quietly become four different things, and the version that applies to you depends entirely on the passport in your pocket. Get the wrong one and you can be denied boarding before you even reach China. This guide sorts every current route by who it's actually for, with the numbers updated to mid-2026.
Start with one distinction or you'll get it wrong
China runs four separate no-visa routes. Two let you fly in and travel the country like any tourist. One only works if you're passing through to somewhere else. One is region-locked. Here's the shape of it:

The trap is the third row. Transit visa-free is not a holiday permit — it only applies when you're flying onward to a different country, and it pins you to a region. People read "China is visa-free now," book a one-way ticket, and get turned away at check-in. Read your row carefully.

Route 1: Unilateral 30-day visa-free (the big one)
This is China deciding, on its own and with no reciprocity required, to wave through ordinary-passport holders from 50 countries. No application, no fee, no onward ticket. You land, show your passport, get a 30-day stamp, and travel anywhere open to foreigners.
It started small in December 2023 — six countries, 15 days — jumped to a 30-day stay in late 2024, and has widened repeatedly since. The headline change for English-speaking travelers: the United Kingdom and Canada were added on 17 February 2026. If any page still tells British or Canadian tourists they "must use the transit route," it's out of date.
The current 50 (ordinary passports, up to 30 days):
Europe (35): Andorra, Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, Cyprus, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Iceland, Ireland, Italy, Latvia, Liechtenstein, Luxembourg, Malta, Monaco, Montenegro, Netherlands, North Macedonia, Norway, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Russia, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, United Kingdom

Asia (7): Bahrain, Brunei, Japan, Kuwait, Oman, South Korea, Saudi Arabia

Oceania (2): Australia, New Zealand

Americas (6): Argentina, Brazil, Canada, Chile, Peru, Uruguay

One thing to know before a later trip: this is still officially a trial. It's currently authorized through 31 December 2026 and has been rolled forward before, but it isn't permanent. Check the date holds before you book anything for 2027.
Route 2: Mutual treaty exemptions
This is a separate, older track — countries that signed a two-way visa-waiver treaty with China, so both sides drop the visa. For tourists, the ones that matter are Thailand (30 days, since March 2024), Singapore (30 days, since February 2024), Kazakhstan, and Georgia. Around two dozen countries hold ordinary-passport treaties, and several of them (Singapore, Brunei, the UAE, Qatar) also sit on the unilateral list above — so you just use whichever covers you. At the border it behaves exactly like Route 1: no onward ticket, travel freely.
Route 3: 240-hour (10-day) visa-free transit
This is the route for the big nationalities still left off the lists above — most importantly the United States and India. It replaced the old 72-hour and 144-hour transit schemes on 17 December 2024.
Here's the mechanism. You're flying from Country A, through China, onward to a different Country C. You're allowed to break that journey in China for up to 240 hours — ten days. The onward ticket to that third country is mandatory and it gets checked at the gate and at immigration.
Eligible nationalities: 55. The December 2024 launch list of 54 grew by one when Indonesia was added in June 2025.

Entry points: 65 ports across 24 provinces and municipalities — every major airport you'd realistically land at, plus a growing set of land and sea crossings.

Hong Kong and Macau count as a "third region." So New York → Shanghai → Hong Kong is a valid routing.

You can now move between provinces inside the permitted zone, which is a real upgrade from the old single-city limits.

What still catches people out:
The onward flight has to go to a third country or region — not back to where you came from.

Several areas are off-limits on transit: Tibet, Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia, Ningxia, Qinghai, Gansu, and Jilin.

There are no extensions. If your plan needs 11 days, you need a real visa.

Register your stay within 24 hours of arrival. A hotel does this for you automatically; if you're with friends, register at the local police station.

Route 4: Hainan, the Greater Bay Area, and cruises
Hainan, the island. Hainan province runs its own 30-day visa-free policy for 61 countries — and this list includes the US, UK, Canada, and Russia. Fly into Haikou or Sanya and you can stay up to 30 days. The hard limit: you cannot leave Hainan for the mainland without a proper visa. It's a beach holiday, not a side door to Beijing.
Greater Bay Area tour groups. A foreign tour group booked through a Hong Kong- or Macau-registered travel agency can enter nine Pearl River Delta cities — Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Foshan, Dongguan, Zhongshan, Jiangmen, Zhaoqing, and Huizhou — for up to 144 hours, six days, visa-free. This is the arrangement behind the Hong Kong day-trips to Guangzhou.
Cruises. Foreign passengers on an international cruise calling at ports like Shanghai, Tianjin, Qingdao, or Xiamen can go ashore visa-free for up to 15 days, as long as they leave again with the ship.

US and Indian passport holders are the big exception. For a normal China holiday you still need a visa — the 10-year L tourist visa is the common choice. Your only no-visa options are the 240-hour transit, if you're genuinely going onward to a third country, or a Hainan beach trip.
Check before you fly — the one official source
The rules here move month to month. The unilateral list keeps growing, new ports keep opening, and the old transit schemes are dead. Don't trust a 2023 blog post or a YouTube video about "72-hour transit" — that scheme no longer exists. The single source of truth is China's National Immigration Administration (NIA); its English site lists the current unilateral countries, the transit participants, and the regional policies. Cross-check with your airline too — ground staff can refuse to board you if you don't qualify, so they tend to track the live rules closely.
What you need at the border, whichever route you use
A passport valid for at least six months, with a blank visa page

An onward or return ticket — mandatory for 240-hour transit, smart to carry on any route

Your first night's hotel booking, or a host's address

The arrival and health declaration, filled out on the plane or at an arrivals kiosk

If you're applying for a full visa instead, it's worth getting the photo specs right the first time — a rejected photo is the most common reason applications bounce.
Frequently asked questions
Can US citizens enter China visa-free in 2026? Not for a normal holiday. US nationals aren't on the unilateral or treaty lists. Your no-visa options are the 240-hour transit scheme, if you're flying onward to a third country, or a direct trip to Hainan for a beach-only stay. For anything else you need a visa.
Do I need a visa for a 2-hour layover at Shanghai Pudong? No, as long as you stay airside and don't collect your bags. A same-day transit where you never leave the international area needs nothing. The moment you clear immigration to claim luggage and re-check, you're using the 240-hour transit route and need that stamp.
How does the 240-hour transit stamp work at the airport? At immigration, head for the temporary-entry or transit counter, not the standard visa lane. Show your passport, your onward boarding pass to the third country, and your accommodation details. The officer issues a temporary-entry stamp valid for up to 240 hours within the permitted area.
Can I work remotely during a visa-free stay? Visa-free entry is for tourism, business meetings, family visits, and transit. Answering your own email on a laptop isn't policed in practice, but performing paid work for a Chinese company is not allowed on any visa-free route.
What happens if I overstay? Fines run ¥500 per day, capped at ¥10,000, and serious cases can mean 5 to 15 days of detention. An overstay can also get you barred from re-entry for one to ten years. Always plan to leave before your stamp expires, not on the day it does.
Is Tibet covered by visa-free entry? No. The Tibet Autonomous Region needs a separate Tibet Travel Permit no matter what your visa status is, arranged through a licensed tour operator. Being visa-free doesn't change that.
I'm a dual national — which passport do I use? Enter and exit on the same passport, and make it the one that qualifies for visa-free entry. Don't hand over two passports at the counter; immigration treats you as whatever document you present, and a mismatch between entry and exit causes problems on the way out.
The Hainan route deserves more attention than it gets, because it is the one that covers passports the national scheme does not — including the United States. Hainan's own 30-day policy needs no onward third-country ticket and, since the rules changed, no travel agency filing on your behalf either.
