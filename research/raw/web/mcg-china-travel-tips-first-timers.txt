China Travel Tips for First-Timers: 2026 Survival Guide

Your first trip to China is easier in 2026 than it was at any point in the previous decade. Alipay and WeChat Pay now accept foreign credit cards, the 240-hour visa-free transit program covers 57 nationalities, eSIM services handle your data before you even land, and the HSR booking app runs in clean English. The outdated advice floating around travel forums ("bring wads of cash, you need a local sponsor, the internet does not work") is mostly wrong now.
What you actually need is a short, accurate list of the things that do trip up first-timers: which payment method to set up before flying, which VPN works, how the taxi apps handle foreigners, what the trains cost, and the cultural quirks that will matter when you are standing on a Shanghai sidewalk trying to order lunch. Here is that list.

Visa and entry in 2026
Two paths exist. Use the one that matches your trip length and itinerary .
240-hour visa-free transit
Since late 2024, China offers 240 hours (10 days) of visa-free transit for passport holders from 57 countries including the United States, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, every Schengen EU state, Japan, Singapore, Indonesia, Brazil, and Argentina. You qualify if you have a confirmed onward flight to a third country (not your origin), enter through one of 60+ approved ports, and stay within approved provinces (basically most of eastern, central, and southern China including Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Chengdu, Xi'an, Hangzhou, Chongqing).
Perfect for: a 4-day Shanghai stopover on your way Singapore-to-Paris, a long layover in Beijing on a Tokyo-to-London flight, a week in Chengdu on a multi-country Asia trip.
Not valid for: round-trip itineraries (your outbound must go to a different country than your arrival), trips longer than 10 days, multiple entries within a short window.
L tourist visa
For round-trip travelers, longer stays, or multi-entry travelers, apply for the L visa at least 4 weeks before your trip. See our separate complete L visa guide for the COVA application walkthrough. Fees are $140 for US citizens (10-year multi-entry), £85 UK, €126 Schengen, A$109.50 Australian. Processing is 4 business days standard, 2 days express for an extra $20.
Mobile payments: the single most important thing to set up
China is a cashless economy. Restaurants, taxis, shops, metros, vending machines, and even temple donation boxes run on Alipay and WeChat Pay QR codes. Paper yuan still works but fewer and fewer vendors have change for a ¥100 note. Get mobile payments working before you fly.
Alipay with foreign Visa/Mastercard
Since 2023 Alipay lets foreign visitors link a Visa, Mastercard, Amex, Diners, Discover, or JCB card directly to their Alipay account without a Chinese bank account. Transactions under ¥2,000 usually succeed automatically. Over ¥2,000 per transaction some banks decline the foreign merchant code, so split big charges.
Setup steps:
Download Alipay from the App Store or Google Play (yes, it works outside China).

Register with your foreign phone number. Receive SMS verification code.

Complete passport identity verification (photo of passport page, selfie).

In Settings, add a Visa or Mastercard to your payment methods.

Top up Tour Pass if you want a prepaid buffer — minimum $100, maximum $2,000, valid 90 days, roughly 5% service fee.

Ready to pay anywhere.

WeChat Pay
WeChat Pay accepts foreign cards with similar rules as of 2026. Same general setup: install WeChat, register with passport, link card, pay. Some travelers find Alipay's interface easier for first-timers. You only need one of the two, not both. Most vendors accept either.
Cash backup
Withdraw ¥500-1,000 at the airport ATM on arrival as a backup. Chinese ATMs (ICBC, Bank of China, China Construction Bank) accept foreign Visa/Mastercard and Cirrus/Plus cards at a ¥2,500 per-transaction limit. Keep the cash for the few situations where mobile payment fails: small roadside food stalls, rural areas, the occasional temple.
Connectivity: eSIM, local SIM, and roaming
Your home carrier's international roaming works in China but costs $10-15 per day on most US plans. Skip it. Pick one of three options before departure.
eSIM (recommended for trips under 14 days)
Airalo and Holafly sell China-specific eSIM packages you install on your phone before boarding the flight. Airalo's China Unicom eSIM gives you 5GB for 7 days at around $20, 10GB for 15 days at $35. Holafly offers unlimited data packages for slightly more. Both auto-connect the moment you land.
Important detail: some China eSIMs route through Hong Kong servers, which means Google, WhatsApp, Instagram, and Facebook work without a VPN. This is the biggest reason experienced travelers pick eSIMs over local SIMs. Airalo's China Unicom eSIM routes through mainland servers (needs VPN for Google services). Check the product listing before buying.
Local SIM at the airport
Every major airport has a China Unicom or China Mobile shop selling tourist SIMs for around ¥100-200. Show your passport, pay cash or mobile, walk out with a working SIM. Data is fast (5G in Tier 1 cities) and the price per GB is cheap. Downside: Google, WhatsApp, Instagram, YouTube, and Facebook are blocked on a mainland SIM. You need a VPN.
China Mobile roaming via home carrier
For travelers who refuse to mess with eSIM or local SIM, some home carriers partner with China Mobile for decent-rate international plans. T-Mobile's international plan includes China (slow 2G speeds, free). Google Fi works in China. Most European carriers offer ~€10/day for a proper 4G experience.
VPN: essential for Google, WhatsApp, and most Western apps
Google (Search, Maps, Gmail, YouTube, Drive, Photos), WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, Twitter/X, Reddit, Discord, Wikipedia, and the New York Times are blocked on mainland Chinese internet. You need a VPN to access them from a mainland SIM or hotel WiFi.
Working VPNs in 2026:
Astrill ($15/month, most reliable, what expats use)

ExpressVPN ($12/month, works most of the time)

NordVPN ($10/month, works intermittently)

Lightsail VPN (free tier, limited speed)

Install before you arrive. The Chinese app stores do not carry VPN apps, and downloading them inside the country is harder without already having a VPN. Set up Astrill or ExpressVPN at home, test it, then fly.
The eSIM workaround
If you buy a China eSIM that routes through Hong Kong (like Airalo Hong Kong-routed plans), you skip the VPN requirement entirely because your data traffic never touches the mainland firewall. This is why savvy travelers pick HK-routed eSIMs and carry a VPN only as backup.
High-speed rail: the best way to see China
China's HSR network is 45,000+ kilometers long and connects every major city at 300 km/h. A Beijing-Shanghai G train covers 1,318 km in 4.5 hours, cheaper and faster than flying when you count airport security and transfers.
Booking
Use the 12306 English app (official, no platform fee, accepts passport registration) or Trip.com International (small booking fee, polished English interface, 24/7 support). Both pull from the same inventory. Book 3-15 days in advance — tickets go on sale 15 days before departure at 06:00 Beijing time.

Second class is perfectly comfortable for most travelers — 5 seats per row (3+2), reclining, power outlets, luggage racks. Upgrade to first class only on trips over 4 hours if you want extra legroom.
Boarding
Arrive 45 minutes early. Pass security, find the waiting hall, and board when the gate opens 15 minutes before departure. Scan your passport at the electronic gate to enter the platform. No paper ticket needed for G, D, or C trains.
Hotels: how to filter for foreigner-friendly
Chinese hotels register foreign guests with the local police bureau at check-in (a routine 2-minute process). Smaller guesthouses in rural areas sometimes lack the software to do this and turn foreigners away. Stick to properties that explicitly accept foreign guests on Trip.com, Booking.com, or Agoda.
On Trip.com, enable the "Accept Foreign Guests" filter. On Booking.com, stick to 3-star and above in cities. Hotel chains that reliably accept foreigners: Hanting, Jinjiang Inn, Home Inn, Atour, Hilton, Marriott, IHG, all budget and upscale chains.
Bring your passport to check-in. The front desk photocopies the photo page and visa, enters your info into the national registration system, and gives you a room key. The whole thing takes 5 minutes.
Language: apps that work
You do not need Mandarin to travel China in 2026 but you do need three apps.
Pleco (Chinese dictionary)
Free. Offline. Handwriting recognition. Camera-based character recognition. The gold standard for English-Chinese lookup. Install before you fly.
Google Translate
The camera feature — point at a menu and see English overlay — is the single most useful travel app in China. Download the Chinese language pack before you fly so it works offline. If you use a mainland SIM without a VPN, Google Translate does not work. Plan accordingly.
Amap (Gaode Maps) English version
Google Maps is blocked. Apple Maps partially works. The reliable choice is Amap with the English setting enabled. It shows accurate transit routes, walking directions, and search results for restaurants, hotels, and attractions in English.
Baidu Maps is the other option but has a worse English interface.
Getting around: Didi, metro, and walking
Didi
Didi Chuxing is China's Uber, and since 2018 the app has an English mode with foreign payment support. Install, register with passport, link your Alipay or credit card, and call a car with English destinations. Fares are about half of US Uber prices — ¥25-40 for most in-city rides in Beijing and Shanghai.
Metro
Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Chengdu, Xi'an, Hangzhou, and Wuhan all have extensive metro systems with English signage, English announcements, and English ticket machines. Flat fare of ¥3-9 depending on distance. Buy a single-ride QR code through Alipay's city-specific metro mini-program (different one per city) or use cash at the machines.
Walking
Chinese cities are walkable and safe. Pedestrian crossings are respected in Beijing and Shanghai, less so in smaller cities. Watch for electric scooters which come fast and silent from any direction.
Cultural norms and tipping
Tipping
Not expected and sometimes refused. Restaurants, hotels, taxis, tour guides — none of them require a tip. Higher-end Western hotel restaurants may accept tips from foreign guests without comment. Leaving change on a restaurant table goes into the staff tip pool in some places but is culturally neutral, not expected. If you feel strongly about tipping a guide who showed you a great hutong walk, ¥100-200 cash handed directly with thanks is appropriate.
Dress
Smart casual. Shorts, t-shirts, jeans, and sneakers are fine almost anywhere except temples (cover shoulders and knees) and formal venues. Beijing and Shanghai lean more formal than Southeast Asia. Coastal cities dress better than inland cities.
Public behavior
Talking loudly, pushing in line, and blowing your nose at the table are culturally neutral in China. Locals do all three. Do not be shocked. Spitting in public has declined in first-tier cities but still happens. Smoking is banned in most indoor spaces.
Photography of strangers
Ask first. A simple gesture with the camera and a smile is enough. Most Chinese people are happy to be photographed in tourist areas. Do not photograph military facilities, police, or government buildings.
Safety: an honest note
China is one of the safest countries in the world for foreign tourists. Violent crime against visitors is vanishingly rare, pickpocketing is uncommon outside crowded tourist zones like the Forbidden City entrance, and walking alone at night in Beijing or Shanghai or Chengdu is safe for solo female travelers.
Real risks to manage:
Traffic. Electric scooters are silent and fast. Look both ways even on sidewalks.

Scams around tourist sites. "Tea ceremony" invitations near Wangfujing, "art student" gallery pitches in Shanghai, fake monks asking for donations at Yonghegong. Politely decline and walk away.

Pollution and weather extremes. Winter smog in northern cities, summer heat and humidity in southern cities. Check AQI on the Amap weather layer.

Overstay. Exit on time. Chinese immigration fines for overstaying are ¥500 per day up to ¥10,000 plus deportation.

FAQ
Is China safe for solo female travelers?
Yes. Tens of thousands of solo female travelers visit China every year without incident. Walking alone at night in Beijing, Shanghai, Chengdu, or Guangzhou is safer than most US or European cities. The main concerns are the same as anywhere: be aware of your surroundings, avoid getting drunk with strangers, and trust your instincts if something feels off.
How much does a 10-day China trip cost in 2026?
Budget backpacker: $80-100 per day including hostel dorm (¥80-150), street food and basic restaurants (¥100), one train or metro ride, one paid attraction, and incidentals. Mid-range: $150-250 per day with a 3-star hotel (¥350-600), sit-down restaurants, taxis, and attractions. Luxury: $400+ per day with 5-star hotels, fine dining, private guides. A 10-day trip for two at mid-range runs roughly $3,000-4,500 excluding international flights.
Do I need to speak any Mandarin?
No, but learn four phrases: "hello" (ni hao), "thank you" (xie xie), "how much" (duo shao qian), and "I don't understand" (wo bu dong). Everything else, your phone handles. Staff at airports, hotels, and major attractions in Tier 1 cities speak functional English. Rural areas rely entirely on your translation app.
What is the best month to visit China for a first trip?
September and October. Weather is mild across most of China (Beijing, Shanghai, Chengdu, Xi'an all comfortable), tourist crowds are moderate outside the October 1-7 national holiday week, and HSR tickets are easier to book than during Spring Festival. April and May are second-best. Avoid July and August for heat, and late January for Chinese New Year chaos.
Can I drink the tap water?
No. Boil it or drink bottled. Hotels provide a kettle and free bottled water in every room. Train stations and most public spaces have free hot water dispensers (for instant noodles and tea) that are safe. Street-side juice and cut fruit from reputable vendors is generally fine. Ice in restaurants in Tier 1 cities is usually made from filtered water; in small towns, skip the ice.
