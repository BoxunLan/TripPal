China has 16 visa types. But 90% of travelers only need one of three letters: L, M, or F. A china l category visa is for tourism, an M is for business, and an F is for non-commercial exchange like research or volunteering. Pick wrong and your $140 application fee is gone. Applying under the wrong category is the fastest way to get rejected.

L, M, and F: The Three You'll Probably Actually Use
Nine out of ten travelers need one of these. Picking wrong is the single most common mistake I see.
L Visa: Tourism (The Default)
The chinese visa types everyone defaults to. A china l category visa is for tourism, visiting friends, sightseeing, or anything that isn't business, study, or work. If you're flying in to see the Great Wall, eat dumplings in Xi'an, and take photos in Shanghai: this is you.
Stay : 30 or 60 days per entry

Validity : 3 months (single) up to 10 years multiple-entry for US and Canadian citizens

Fee : $140 flat for US citizens (reduced from $185 through 31 December 2026); ¥240–¥720 ($34–$102) for others depending on entries

Needed : Passport, COVA form, photo, flight itinerary, hotel bookings

The 10-year multi-entry L is a huge deal if you plan to come back. One application, one fee, ten years of flexibility. I recommend it to any US traveler planning more than one trip in five years.
M Visa: Business (Not F, Not L)
The M visa replaced the old business-half of the F visa back in 2013. Use this if you're going for anything commercial: meetings, negotiations, contracts, trade fairs, sales trips, installation and repair work, or visiting a Chinese supplier.
Stay : 30–60 days per entry

Validity : up to 10 years multi-entry for US/Canada

Needed : Passport, COVA form, photo, and an invitation letter from a Chinese company (the key M visa document)

Fee : $140 for US citizens

If money changes hands, goods are being sold, or a contract is being signed: you want M. Canton Fair? M. Alibaba factory visit? M. Trade show in Shenzhen? M.
F Visa: Non-Commercial Exchange
This is where people get burned. An F visa china is for non-commercial activities: academic research, university lectures, scientific exchanges, cultural performances, NGO work, religious exchange, or volunteering for 90 days or less.
Stay : 30–90 days per entry

Validity : Usually single or double entry, shorter than L/M

Needed : Invitation from a Chinese university, research institute, or approved exchange organization

So here's the thing. If you're a professor giving a free lecture at Tsinghua, it's F. If you're the same professor getting paid by a Chinese company to consult, it's M. The line is money.
Pro tip : When in doubt between F and M, ask yourself one question: "Is anyone paying for something on the Chinese side?" If yes (goods, services, contracts, consulting fees) it's M. If no (pure academic or cultural exchange) it's F.

Z, X, S, and Q: Work, Study, and Family Categories
These are the longer-stay visas. If you're actually moving to China (for a job, a degree, or to be with family), one of these is your letter.
Z Visa: Work
A Z visa is the entry document for anyone employed by a Chinese company. But here's what confuses people: the Z is only good for 30 days . After you land, you apply for a work residence permit within those 30 days, and that's your actual long-term stay document.
Needed : Work Permit Notification letter from Chinese employer, medical exam, no-criminal-record check, degree verification

Fee : $140 for US citizens

After arrival : Register with local PSB within 24 hours, apply for residence permit within 30 days

X1 and X2: Student
Two student visas depending on program length:
X1 : Programs longer than 180 days (full degree, year abroad, long research). Single entry, 30 days, then residence permit. You need the official JW202 or JW201 form from the Chinese school.

X2 : Programs under 180 days (summer language intensive, short exchange). Treated more like a regular short-stay visa. No residence permit needed.

S1 and S2: Family of Z/X Holders
The china s visa is for foreign family members of people already in China on Z (work) or X (study) visas. Spouse, parents, kids under 18.
S1 : Staying longer than 180 days. You'll apply for a residence permit after arrival.

S2 : Staying 180 days or less. Available as multiple entry (up to 10 years for US/Canada citizens).

If your husband works in Shanghai and you're visiting for a month, that's S2. If you're moving to join him for a year, that's S1.
Q1 and Q2: Family of Chinese Citizens or Permanent Residents
Looks like S but it's a totally different category. Q visas are for family of Chinese citizens or permanent residents (not foreigners living in China).
Q1 : Long-term reunion (>180 days), turns into residence permit

Q2 : Short visits (≤180 days), available as 10-year multi-entry for US/Canada

Q vs S confusion : I get this every month. If your relative is a Chinese national or holds a Chinese green card, you need Q. If your relative is a foreigner working or studying in China, you need S. Applying under the wrong family category is an automatic rejection. Immigration checks the sponsor's passport.
D, C, G, J, R, and the Port Visa (The Rare Ones)
Most readers won't need these. Quick tour:
D Visa (Permanent residence): aka the Chinese green card. Notoriously hard. Requires major investment, Chinese spouse + 5 years residence, or top-tier talent status.

C Visa (Crew): Pilots, flight attendants, ship crews, international truck drivers.

G Visa (Transit): Honestly? If you qualify for 240-hour visa-free transit , use that instead. It's free.

J1 / J2 Visa (Journalist): J1 for resident posts over 180 days, J2 for temporary assignments 30 days or less.

R Visa (High-level talent): Scientists and specialists China actively wants. Since 2024 it's multi-entry, 5 or 10 years, with expedited processing. Your host institution handles it.

Port Visa : Emergency on-arrival visa at select ports (Shanghai Pudong, Beijing Capital, Guangzhou Baiyun, Xiamen, Shenzhen, Zhuhai). 5-day single entry, ¥168–¥510 ($24–$72). Only for urgent business. Don't plan around it.

Which China Visa Do You Need? The Decision Tree
The simplest way to choose. Match your purpose to a letter:
Sightseeing, visiting friends, photos → L (or 30-day visa-free if eligible)

Business meeting, trade fair, contract signing, factory visit → M

Research, academic lecture, cultural exchange, NGO, unpaid volunteer ≤90 days → F

Job at a Chinese company → Z

Studying >6 months → X1

Short language course or summer program <6 months → X2

Visiting your foreign spouse/parent who works or studies in China → S1 (long) or S2 (short)

Visiting your Chinese-citizen family or PR-holding relatives → Q1 (long) or Q2 (short)

Working as crew on an international flight or ship → C

Just transiting (and 240-hour doesn't work) → G

Reporting as a foreign journalist → J1 or J2

Moving to China permanently → D

Invited as a top-tier specialist or scientist → R

Emergency, no time to apply at a consulate → Port visa

China Multiple Entry Visa: When It's Worth It
A china multiple entry visa lets you enter as many times as you want during the validity period. Worth it? Depends on how often you plan to come back.
Single entry L/M : 3 months validity, ~$34 (non-US). One trip.

Double entry L/M : 6 months validity, ~$51 (non-US). Two trips.

Multiple entry 6 months : ~$68 (non-US). Unlimited in 6 months.

Multiple entry 12 months : ~$102 (non-US). Unlimited in 12 months.

10-year multi-entry L/M/S2/Q2 : US and Canada only, $140 flat at the current reduced rate. Best value if you'll come back even twice.

The math. A US consultant making three trips a year for five years would pay $140 × 15 = $2,100 in single-entry fees. One 10-year multi-entry visa? $140 total. That is $1,960 saved by ticking the right box.
Pro tip : Max stay per entry on the 10-year visa is usually 60 days , not 10 years. People confuse validity with stay length. A 10-year visa means you can enter for the next 10 years. Each visit still caps at 60 days.

US Citizens: Reciprocity and the 10-Year Visa
Quick section because US rules are different.
Under the US-China visa reciprocity agreement, US passport holders pay a flat $140 for any visa category (single, double, or multiple entry). Same fee. This is set by travel.state.gov and hasn't changed in years.
The perk: US and Canadian citizens can get 10-year multiple-entry L, M, S2, and Q2 visas. Most other nationalities max out at 12 months. If you're planning repeat trips, this is the reason to pick the right category the first time.

FAQ
What's the difference between F visa and M visa in China?
F is non-commercial exchange; M is commercial business. Research, academic lectures, cultural events, NGO work, and unpaid volunteering go under F. Trade fairs, contract negotiations, sales meetings, and factory inspections go under M. If money changes hands on the Chinese side, it's M.
Can I change my visa type after I arrive in China?
Usually no. The category is fixed when issued. You can extend most visas at the local PSB Exit-Entry Bureau, but switching from L to Z means leaving China and reapplying. Exception: Z and X visas can be converted to work or study residence permits locally.
Does China still issue 10-year multiple entry visas in 2026?
Yes. The 10-year multi-entry L, M, S2, and Q2 visas are still available for US and Canadian citizens. $140 flat at the current reduced rate. Max stay per entry is 60 days.
What happens if I apply under the wrong visa type?
Best case: the consulate calls you to correct it and you pay again. Worst case: rejected outright with no refund, or approved but denied entry at the border when immigration checks your purpose. The F-instead-of-M mistake is the classic one.
Can I use a port visa to enter China as a tourist?
No. Plan around a regular L visa instead. Port visas are for emergencies and unexpected business, not tourism. You need a clear reason, a sponsoring Chinese entity, and you'll only get 5 days.

Bottom line: pick your letter based on what you're actually going to do in China, not what's easiest to apply for. For 90% of travelers that means a china l category visa (tourism), M (business), or F (non-commercial exchange). Write down your purpose in one sentence, match it to the decision tree above, and you'll be fine.
