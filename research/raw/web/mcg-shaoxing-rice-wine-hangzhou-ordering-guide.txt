Most foreigners who try Shaoxing rice wine for the first time do it in a Hangzhou restaurant, order whatever the waiter points at, and decide it tastes "like cooking sherry mixed with soy sauce." That is not Shaoxing rice wine's fault. That is the fault of ordering the wrong bottle at the wrong temperature with the wrong food.
Done right, huangjiu (黄酒) is one of the most layered drinking experiences in Chinese cuisine — warm, round, faintly sweet, with the kind of long finish that makes red-braised pork disappear off the plate. And Hangzhou, one hour from Shaoxing by train, is where you can drink it best without leaving the Yangtze Delta. This guide teaches you how to order it properly in 2026.

What Shaoxing rice wine actually is
Shaoxing rice wine is Chinese yellow wine — huangjiu in Mandarin (黄酒, literally "yellow wine") — made from glutinous rice, water from Shaoxing's Jianhu Lake, and wheat-based qu starter. It ferments for months in clay urns, then ages anywhere from one to twenty years. Alcohol content is typically 14–18% — closer to sake than to Western wine, but with far more body.
The Shaoxing region has produced it for 2,500 years. In 2006 it was one of the first products registered under China's Geographical Indication system, which means "Shaoxing wine" is legally protected the way Champagne is in France. A bottle labelled Shaoxing Huangjiu must come from Shaoxing.
The five varieties you will see on menus
Every Hangzhou restaurant of any quality will stock some subset of these. If your menu has only "rice wine" with no variety specified, it is probably cheap Yuanhong, and you should ask for the proper list.
1. Yuanhong (元红)
The driest style. Golden amber, straightforward grain nose, almost no sweetness. Classic everyday drinking wine. Lower residual sugar (under 15 g/L) makes it the sharpest of the five. Good match for salty Shanghainese cold dishes and fresh seafood.
2. Jiafan (加饭)
Literally "added rice" — made by adding extra glutinous rice to the mash, which gives more body and faint residual sweetness. This is the most popular style for general drinking in Hangzhou and Shanghai. Medium-bodied, slight honey notes, long finish. If you order one variety and do not know what you want, order Jiafan.
3. Shanniang (善酿)
Made by fermenting rice in older Yuanhong wine instead of water. This double-fermentation produces a richer, slightly sweeter result — more like a medium Oloroso than a dry sherry. Good for slow sipping after dinner. Often served warm.
4. Xiangxue (香雪)
The sweet style. Made with glutinous rice distillate added during fermentation, which arrests yeast activity and leaves significant residual sugar (100 g/L+). Alcohol is higher, around 18%. Dessert-style. Pair with walnuts, candied lotus root, or sweet rice dumplings.
5. Huadiao (花雕)
Not strictly a separate variety — Huadiao (花雕) refers to aged Jiafan wine stored in decorated urns. The aging (5, 8, 10, 15, 20 years) deepens color toward mahogany and adds complex dried-fruit and nutty notes. This is the premium tier. A good 10-year Huadiao from Shaoxing's best producers is what you order when you want to show off.

How to order it in a Hangzhou restaurant
Ordering by the pot
Most mid-range Hangzhou restaurants serve huangjiu by the pot (一壶 yi hu), which is usually a 250ml or 500ml clay or ceramic pot. The pot is either warmed over a small stove with a brazier, or served room temperature with ice on the side. Pot prices range from ¥20 for basic Yuanhong to ¥100+ for aged Huadiao.
Phonetic ordering:
"yi hu jiafan" (一壶加饭) — a pot of Jiafan

"yi hu huadiao, re de" (一壶花雕, 热的) — a pot of Huadiao, warm

"yi hu yuanhong, liang de" (一壶元红, 凉的) — a pot of Yuanhong, cold

Ordering by the bottle
Finer restaurants sell by the 500ml bottle, often displayed in a wood or clay cellar along the wall. A bottle of everyday Jiafan runs ¥50–100; aged Huadiao bottles from respected producers like Guyue Longshan (古越龙山), Pagoda (塔牌), or Kuaijishan (会稽山) run ¥150–400.
Warm vs cold: the seasonal rule
Hangzhou locals warm their huangjiu in winter (November–March) and drink it cool or room temperature in summer (July–September). Spring and autumn are borderline and depend on the dish.
Warming technique
The traditional method is to stand a small ceramic pot in a bowl of hot water — a kind of bain-marie — until the wine reaches about 40°C. Some restaurants use a brass brazier with a small flame under the pot. The goal is warm, not hot: too much heat drives off the alcohol and the subtle aromatics.
You can also drop a dried red date, a slice of ginger, or a goji berry into the warming pot. This is traditional in winter and adds a gentle herbal warmth that pairs well with red-braised meat.
Cold serving
In summer, Yuanhong and Jiafan are often drunk chilled or at room temperature. Good Hangzhou restaurants will bring a small carafe at 12–14°C, similar to a light sake. It refreshes the palate between bites of freshwater prawn or Longjing tea-smoked fish.
Where to drink Shaoxing rice wine in Hangzhou
1. Qinghefang old street (清河坊)
The historic commercial street near Wushan Square has several traditional drinking houses. "Lao Shaoxing Jiuguan" (老绍兴酒馆) is the obvious one — they pour everything from ¥15 Yuanhong to ¥300 aged Huadiao, and they warm pots on a brass stove at the counter. Food is Hangzhou-Shaoxing cross-style: drunken chicken, smoked fish, pickled vegetables.
2. Hefang Street (河坊街)
One block over from Qinghefang, Hefang Street is more tourist-heavy but holds several respected huangjiu restaurants. Look for signs with 黄酒 (huangjiu) prominent on the banner and clay urns on display near the entrance. Prices are slightly higher than Qinghefang but the quality is comparable.
3. West Lake restaurants
Waipoji (外婆家, "Grandma's Kitchen") is the national chain every Hangzhou visitor ends up at. They serve a decent Jiafan for ¥38/pot alongside their signature sweet-and-sour pork and braised lion's head meatballs. It is not the best huangjiu in town, but it is accessible, affordable, and consistent.
For finer pours, Green Tea Restaurant (绿茶餐厅) and Kuiyuanguan (奎元馆, the famous 1860s noodle house) carry better Huadiao selections. Kuiyuanguan in particular pairs huangjiu with their shrimp and eel noodle — a classic combination.
4. Longjing village and Manjuelong
Out in the tea hills around Longjing village, small farmhouse restaurants specialise in simple Hangzhou home cooking — Dongpo pork, spring bamboo, wild mushrooms — with huangjiu served warm in winter. Meituan reviews (translate with Chrome) will show you which are pouring the aged bottles.
Classic food pairings
Huangjiu is made to pair with food. Unlike Western wine culture, where the drink is the focus, Shaoxing wine is an amplifier for the dish.
1. Drunken chicken (醉鸡)
Drunken chicken is chicken poached and then bathed overnight in aged Huadiao wine. Ironically, the best thing to drink with it is more huangjiu. The wine in the marinade and the wine in the glass overlap and deepen. Order a pot of Jiafan or aged Huadiao.
2. Red-braised pork (东坡肉)
Hangzhou's signature dish, slow-cooked pork belly in sweet soy and wine. The sweetness and umami of the pork want a richer, slightly sweet huangjiu. Jiafan or Shanniang, served warm.
3. Freshwater shrimp with Longjing tea (龙井虾仁)
Delicate, green, tea-perfumed. Too-heavy wine overpowers it. Order a light Yuanhong, served cool or room temperature.
4. Braised lion's head meatballs (狮子头)
Giant pork meatballs in clear broth. Warm Jiafan is the classic match — the wine's light sweetness balances the rich meat.
5. Smoked Yellow River fish (烟熏鳜鱼)
Strong smoky flavors want a bolder wine. Aged Huadiao, warm in winter or lightly chilled in summer.

For most travellers, a ¥50 pot of Jiafan with a plate of drunken chicken and red-braised pork is the correct starting experience. It is not expensive. It is the gateway drink that opens the rest of Chinese yellow wine culture.
Buying Shaoxing wine to take home
The legal limit
You can carry Shaoxing wine in your check-in luggage on international flights home, subject to your destination's alcohol import limits (typically 1–2 litres duty-free). Sealed ceramic urns travel surprisingly well if cushioned.
Where to buy in Hangzhou

Frequently asked questions
Is Shaoxing rice wine the same as sake?
No. Both are rice-based fermented drinks but the processes differ. Sake uses koji mold and polished rice, ferments cool, is typically drier. Shaoxing huangjiu uses qu (a wheat-based starter), unpolished glutinous rice, ferments warmer, and ages for years to develop depth. Huangjiu is darker in color and richer in body.
Is huangjiu strong?
Moderate. Most Shaoxing varieties sit at 14–18% alcohol, similar to heavy red wine or port. The sweet Xiangxue can reach 18–20%. It does not hit you as hard as baijiu (Chinese grain spirit at 40–53%), but it does accumulate — two pots over a dinner is a real experience.
Can I drink it straight or should I mix it?
Straight, always. Shaoxing wine is not a cocktail base — mixing it with soda or juice destroys the fermentation flavors you paid for. The traditional addition is a warming element like dried dates, ginger, or goji berries, which complement rather than mask the wine.
What does "cooking wine" at my Western supermarket have to do with this?
The cheap cooking wine sold in Asian aisles at Western supermarkets is labelled "Shaoxing cooking wine" but is usually a lower-grade Yuanhong with added salt to make it non-drinkable (for beverage-tax reasons). Real huangjiu for drinking has no added salt and tastes completely different. Do not judge Shaoxing wine by what you have cooked with.
Is it OK for the restaurant to warm my wine on a flame stove?
Yes — that is traditional. The stove is a small charcoal brazier or electric warmer, and the pot sits in water so the wine never boils. If the pot is directly on a flame with no water bath, ask them to use the bain-marie method instead. The wine should be warm, not hot.
Can vegetarians and pescatarians drink huangjiu?
Yes. Shaoxing rice wine is fermented from rice, water, and wheat qu. No animal products at any stage. Some aged Huadiao passes through egg-white fining for clarity, but most do not.
Rice wine is a Jiangnan habit rather than a Chinese one, which is part of why ordering it well marks you out — the regional cuisine guide explains where Zhejiang cooking sits and why it is mild by design. The other local drink worth learning properly is tea: the Jingshan temple trail outside Hangzhou is where matcha came from, which almost no English guide mentions. And Suzhou is 25 minutes away when you want a change of city.
