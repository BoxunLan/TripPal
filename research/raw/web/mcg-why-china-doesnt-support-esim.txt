The Real Reason China Blocked eSIM for Over a Decade
Look, I'll be honest with you. This isn't just a technology problem. It's a control problem.
Since 2015, Chinese law requires every SIM card to be registered to a verified, government-issued ID. You walk into a China Mobile store, hand over your ID card, they scan your face, and that SIM is now permanently tied to you. China has 1.7 billion mobile subscriptions running through this system, according to MIIT data. Every single one tracked.
eSIM breaks that model. With eSIM, you can activate a phone plan remotely, switch carriers in seconds, and do it all without setting foot in a store. From the Chinese government's perspective, that's a problem.
Here's what's really going on:
Real-name registration enforcement : Physical SIMs require in-person ID verification at a carrier store. eSIM's remote provisioning skips that step entirely.

Surveillance infrastructure : Every local SIM creates a direct link between your device, your identity, and your location. eSIM makes that link harder to maintain.

Data sovereignty : Locally-issued SIMs route all traffic through Chinese networks where the Great Firewall does its filtering. International eSIMs can route traffic through foreign servers instead.

Carrier lock-in : China Mobile alone has 980 million+ subscribers . All three carriers benefited from physical SIM stickiness. Harder to switch means less customer churn.

I spent an afternoon at a China Mobile store in Shanghai last year helping a friend from Australia get a local SIM. The process took 40 minutes. Face scan, passport copy, phone number selection, system registration. The staff member apologized three times for the wait. That level of verification is the whole point, and eSIM skips it.

What Changed in October 2025 (And Why It Doesn't Help You Yet)
So here's the thing. China did approve eSIM for smartphones. The headlines made it sound like everything changed overnight.
In October 2025, the MIIT (Ministry of Industry and Information Technology) approved nationwide eSIM smartphone trials. All three carriers -- China Mobile, China Unicom, and China Telecom -- started offering eSIM activation.
But here's what the headlines left out:
In-person ID verification is still required. You visit a carrier store, show your Chinese national ID, complete face verification. The eSIM part just means you don't get a physical card at the end.

Only 2 iPhone models are supported in mainland China as of April 2026: the iPhone 17e (model A3635) and iPhone Air (model A3518). That's it. Per Apple's eSIM carrier support page , no other iPhone models sold in China have eSIM hardware.

Foreign passport holders are effectively excluded. The system is designed around Chinese national ID cards. Can you buy a domestic eSIM in China as a tourist? I'm not 100% sure it's technically impossible, but I haven't met anyone who's pulled it off.

Counterpoint Research projects 17 million eSIM-capable smartphones in China by end of 2026, growing to 125 million by 2030 . Globally, about 48% of smartphone sales will be eSIM-enabled by 2026. China is catching up, but slowly.
My honest take: give it 2-3 years before domestic China eSIM support actually matters for tourists. Maybe longer.
The iPhone Thing That Confuses Everyone
This trips up so many people. Let me clear it up.
iPhones sold in mainland China have dual physical nano-SIM slots and no eSIM hardware . None. Apple designed a completely different internal layout for the Chinese market to comply with local regulations. So if your Chinese friend says "my iPhone doesn't have eSIM," they're right. It literally can't.
iPhones bought outside China (US, UK, Europe, Australia, basically everywhere else) have eSIM capability built in. Your phone from home works fine with an international eSIM in China.
The two exceptions: the iPhone 17e and iPhone Air sold in China do include eSIM hardware. They're the first Chinese-market iPhones to support it. But that only matters if you bought your phone in China.
So if you're traveling to China with your own phone from overseas: your eSIM works . You install an international eSIM before your flight and have data the moment you land.
Quick check: go to Settings > Cellular (or Settings > Mobile Data) on your iPhone. If you see "Add eSIM" or "Set Up Cellular Plan," you're good. Takes 10 seconds.
What Travelers Should Actually Do
Okay, the practical part. You know why China avoids eSIM devices. Now here's what to do about it.
Option 1: International eSIM (What I Recommend for Most People)
Buy one before you leave home. Install it. Land in China with data already working. Setup takes about 5 minutes on your couch. If you're visiting China visa-free , you've got up to 240 hours -- more than enough time to burn through 10GB.
The big advantage that gets undersold: most international eSIMs bypass the Great Firewall . Google, WhatsApp, Instagram, YouTube -- they all work without a VPN. Your traffic routes through the eSIM provider's international network, not through Chinese infrastructure where content filtering happens.

Option 2: Physical SIM at the Airport
The old-school approach. You can still buy a China prepaid SIM card for tourist use at any airport. Find a China Mobile or China Unicom counter, hand over your passport, and walk out with a working SIM.
Airport tourist packs : ¥100-200 ($14-28) for 7-30 days with 10-20GB

Biggest drawback : local SIMs route through the Great Firewall. Google, WhatsApp, Instagram? All blocked. You'll need a VPN on top, and VPNs in China can be hit-or-miss.

FAQ: eSIM in China
Does China Have eSIM Support Now?
Sort of. Since October 2025, China allows eSIM on smartphones through all three major carriers. But activation requires in-person verification with a Chinese national ID card. Only the iPhone 17e and iPhone Air are supported in mainland China. Can you buy an eSIM in China as a foreigner? Technically the infrastructure exists, but the verification system is designed for Chinese national IDs. For foreign tourists, domestic eSIM isn't a realistic option yet.
Can I Use My Foreign eSIM in China?
Yes. International eSIMs from providers like Nomad, Airalo, and SimOptions work in China through roaming agreements with Chinese carriers. Your foreign phone's eSIM hardware works normally here. You just can't get a local Chinese eSIM plan as a tourist.
Do International eSIMs Bypass the Great Firewall?
Most do, and that's their single biggest advantage over local SIMs. Your data routes through the provider's international network, so Google, WhatsApp, and Instagram typically work without a VPN. I say "typically" because some smaller providers are less reliable. Check recent reviews before buying.
Does My Chinese iPhone Have eSIM?
Almost certainly not. iPhones sold in mainland China have dual physical nano-SIM slots and no eSIM hardware. The only exceptions are the iPhone 17e (model A3635) and iPhone Air (model A3518), released after the October 2025 policy change. Every other Chinese-market iPhone? No eSIM. Period.
What's Cheaper: eSIM or Physical SIM?
A physical SIM from a carrier store downtown is cheaper, starting at ¥50 ($7) for basic plans. But an international eSIM saves you the trip to the store, the registration process, and the Great Firewall headache. For most people, $12-18 for an international eSIM is worth the convenience.
Will China Ever Offer Tourist eSIM?
Probably. The domestic eSIM infrastructure is now live. But extending it to foreign passport holders with simplified verification? That's a regulatory question, not a technical one. Based on how slowly Chinese telecom policy moves, I'd guess 2028-2029 at the earliest for any tourist-friendly option. To be fair, I've been wrong about Chinese tech timelines before.

So why does China avoid eSIM devices? Government control, plain and simple. But the workaround isn't hard: get an international eSIM before you fly, install it at home, and land with data already working. Plan the rest of your trip around the best months to visit and you're set.
