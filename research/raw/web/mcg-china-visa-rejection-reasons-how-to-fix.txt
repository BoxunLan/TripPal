A China visa rejection is not the end of the road. Many refusals trace back to problems you can fix before a second attempt, and one status that looks like a rejection is not a refusal at all: in the online system, "Rejected and to be modified" means the embassy or consulate wants corrections or extra documents (see the last section). If you are trying to figure out what happened, this guide walks through ten common reasons why your visa application got rejected, what each one actually means, and exactly how to fix it before you reapply.

Reason 1: incomplete or inconsistent COVA form
A common reason why your China visa application got rejected in 2026 is an incomplete or inconsistent COVA (China Online Visa Application) form. The system checks your photo and reads your passport page automatically, and in the US system, staff then review the whole form online before you hand in your passport. Missing travel history, a blank or abbreviated employer name, or dates that do not match your documents can get the form sent back or the visa refused.
How to fix it. Refill the entire COVA form from scratch. Do not copy-paste from your old form because errors propagate. Check every field against the documents you will submit. Common trouble spots: work history for the past five years (full company name, no abbreviations), your highest level of education, family details (if you choose "Not Applicable", you must add an explanation), whether you have been to China or held a Chinese visa, and the countries you visited in the past 12 months. The COVA form is the load-bearing document.
Another easy slip is swapping your surname and given name. The COVA form must match your passport bio page exactly, and filling the Last name (姓) and First name (名) fields in reverse order creates an inconsistency. You can fill in the form in English or Chinese. Once you submit it, you cannot edit it yourself: to fix a mistake before the review, you cancel the application and file a new one, and cancellations are limited (see below).
Reason 2: photo does not meet China visa photo requirements
Chinese visa photo specs differ from US passport photos, and the online form checks your upload against them. The current specs, published by China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs:

Requirement

Dimensions

33mm wide by 48mm tall (printed); 354 x 472 to 420 x 560 pixels (digital)

Background

White or close to white, no border, no shadows

15-22mm wide, 28-33mm from chin to crown (printed photo)

Expression

Neutral, mouth closed, both eyes open, ears visible

Head position

Facing the camera, turned or tilted no more than 20° sideways or 25° up or down

Glasses

Allowed only with untinted lenses, no glare, and frames that do not cover the eyes

Hair

Cannot cover eyebrows, eyes, or ears

Head covering

Only for religious reasons, and it must not cover facial features

Clothing

Not the same color as the background

File (digital)

JPEG, 40-120 KB

Print quality

Color, no damage or marks

Age of photo

Taken within last 6 months

How to fix it. Go to a dedicated passport photo service, not a drugstore photo booth. Ask for a China visa photo, 33 by 48 millimeters, and take off your glasses if there is any chance of glare. For the COVA upload, use a JPEG between 354 x 472 and 420 x 560 pixels and 40-120 KB; the system checks it as you upload. Do not use a phone selfie with a white wall.
If your upload keeps failing the automatic check, you can still complete the rest of the form. Bring a printed photo that meets the specs when you hand in your passport.
Reason 3: insufficient financial proof
China's Exit and Entry Administration Law lists being unable to cover your expenses during your stay as a ground for refusing a visa, and consular officers can ask for more documents. Neither the US nor the UK tourist-visa checklist sets a minimum bank balance, and no official daily figure is published.
How to fix it. If money may have been the issue, add financial proof when you reapply: recent bank statements showing a stable balance, pay stubs, an employer letter confirming salary and leave approval, your latest tax return, and evidence of commitments back home (property deed, rental lease, enrolled school). A large deposit just before you apply can look like borrowed money, so steady income over several months makes a stronger case. In the US system, upload extra documents under "Anything else you want to declare".
Reason 4: unclear or incomplete itinerary
Whether you need an itinerary depends on where you apply. Since January 1, 2024, tourist-visa applicants in the US no longer have to submit flight bookings, hotel reservations, an itinerary, or an invitation letter, though officers decide case by case and can still ask for more. Other checklists still require them: the UK list, for example, asks for a round-trip flight booking and hotel reservations, or an invitation letter.
How to fix it. If your checklist asks for travel documents, or an officer requests them, build a day-by-day itinerary and submit it as a cover letter or itinerary page. Include entry date and port of entry, each city with check-in/check-out dates, hotel name and address for every night, inter-city transport (HSR train numbers, flight numbers), and exit date and port of exit. Use Trip.com or Ctrip for refundable hotel bookings and attach the confirmations. Make sure the dates match the ones in your COVA form.
Reason 5: prior visa rejections not disclosed
The application form asks whether you have ever been refused a Chinese visa or refused entry into China, and whether a Chinese visa of yours has ever been cancelled. It also asks which countries you visited in the past 12 months and whether you hold valid visas from other countries. Leaving out a past refusal is a false statement, and China's Exit and Entry Administration Law lists fraud in a visa application as a ground for refusal.
How to fix it. Disclose every past Chinese visa refusal when you reapply, and attach a short written explanation (one page, signed, dated) covering:
When and where you applied, and when you were refused

What you believe caused it (consulates do not have to give a reason)

What has changed since then to address that reason

Honesty is the only cure here: the form's declaration warns that false, misleading, or incomplete statements may lead to refusal. In the US system, upload your explanation under "Anything else you want to declare".
Reason 6: passport issues (blank pages, expiration)
For a US application, your passport needs more than six months of remaining validity and at least two blank visa pages; the UK checklist also asks for at least six months of validity and blank visa pages. If your passport falls short, the US embassy's answer is to renew it before you apply.
How to fix it. Count blank visa pages (Amendments and Endorsements pages do not count). Check expiration; if six months or less remain, renew first. If damaged (water stain, torn corner), consider renewing. Renewing a US passport takes weeks, even with expedited service, so factor this into your timeline before reapplying.
Reason 7: past China overstay on file
The application form asks whether you have ever entered China illegally, overstayed, or worked illegally, so an overstay of even one day comes up again when you reapply. Under China's Exit and Entry Administration Law, an illegal stay can bring a warning or, in serious cases, a fine of 500 yuan a day up to 10,000 yuan, or 5 to 15 days of detention.
How to fix it. Answer the question truthfully and explain what happened. Attach any paperwork you received from the Chinese exit-entry authorities, evidence of the reason (a flight cancellation notice or a medical record, for example), and a short statement committing to follow your visa terms. If you were repatriated or deported, the law sets an entry ban: one to five years from the date of repatriation, or ten years from the date of deportation. A new visa application cannot shorten it.
Reason 8: sensitive profession flags
The application form asks for your work history for the past five years, any military service, and whether you have worked for professional, social, or charitable organizations. Consular officers weigh those answers against the purpose of your trip. Journalists are the clearest case: reporting in China needs a J1 visa (resident correspondents) or a J2 visa (short reporting trips), not a tourist visa.
How to fix it. Apply for the correct category (J1 or J2 for reporting, not L). If the trip is personal, include a letter from your employer or institution explaining your role and confirming the purpose of the trip. Do not conceal your profession: the form's declaration warns that false, misleading, or incomplete statements may lead to refusal.
The form also asks whether you have a criminal record in China or any other country. Answer it truthfully and attach a short explanation of the circumstances.
Reason 9: employer letter is incomplete or weak
Neither the US nor the UK tourist-visa checklist asks for an employer letter, but officers can request more documents, and a letter is the simplest way to show your job, income, and approved leave.
How to fix it. Your employer letter should be printed on company letterhead with physical address, phone, and email, signed by HR or a direct manager with a named title, recently dated, and explicit about your position, start date, salary, and approved leave dates. It should confirm your job is held for you during the trip and be written in English.
A template that works: "This letter confirms that [Name] has been employed as [Title] at [Company] since [Start Date]. Current annual salary is [Amount]. We have approved [Name]'s leave from [Start Date] to [End Date] for personal travel to China, after which [Name] will return to full-time employment at our company." Self-employed applicants should submit a business license, recent tax returns, and an accountant's letter.
Reason 10: the 10-year multi-entry visa reapplication trap
US citizens with expired 10-year multi-entry China visas sometimes assume the renewal is a formality and submit a minimal application. There is no renewal track: a new visa means a new application, checked against the current requirements like any other. Treat it as a fresh application and submit every document the checklist asks for, not just your old visa and new passport.
If you renewed your passport but your old visa is still valid, you may not need a new visa at all. The US embassy says that if your name, gender, date of birth, and nationality have not changed and your trip matches the visa type, you can travel with both passports: the new one and the old one with the visa. If any of those details changed, or your trip has a different purpose, apply for a new visa.
How long to wait before reapplying
The US embassy's published rules set no waiting period after a refusal, so you can reapply as soon as you are ready. Sending the same application again is likely to get the same result, though. The right approach is:
Take the time you need to gather stronger documents.

Fix every reason you suspect caused the rejection (not just the one most likely).

Update whatever may have caused the problem: a new photo, corrected form answers, or extra documents such as bank statements or an employer letter.

Apply through the embassy or consulate that covers where you live. US missions ask you to confirm that you live in their consular district, so switching districts to get a different reviewer is not an option.

Many sites repeat a "3-month cooling-off period," but the embassy's published guidance contains no such rule. What it does limit is cancellations: a submitted COVA form cannot be edited, only cancelled and filed again, and you can cancel at most twice within 90 days. Going over that limit can stop you from resubmitting, so check every field before you submit.
When to use a visa agent instead of reapplying yourself
A China visa agent (not just a mail-in service) can be worth the fee in these situations:
You have been rejected twice

You have a past overstay, prior ban, or complicated background

You work in a sensitive profession

You live far from the embassy or consulate that covers your state: in the US, you or an agent must hand in the passport at the visa office, and the embassy does not accept mailed applications

You do not have time to perfect the package yourself

If you can hand in the application yourself, reapplying without an agent is usually fine once you fix the root cause.

Frequently asked questions
Will a rejection affect future China visa applications? Future application forms ask whether you have ever been refused a Chinese visa, so you will need to answer yes and explain it. A past refusal is not in itself one of the grounds for refusal listed in China's Exit and Entry Administration Law. Fix the likely cause, disclose the refusal, and explain what changed.
Can I appeal a China visa rejection? No. China's Exit and Entry Administration Law says the visa authorities do not have to give reasons for a refusal, and the review route the law provides covers measures such as detention or repatriation, not visa refusals. Your recourse is a new application that fixes the likely cause.
How do I know why my visa application got rejected? Usually you won't be told. The law does not require the consulate to give a reason, so you have to work it out yourself: compare your application against the ten reasons above, starting with the form, the photo, and your passport.
Can I travel to Hong Kong or Macau with a rejected China visa? Yes, if your passport qualifies for visa-free entry there. Hong Kong and Macau set their own entry rules, separate from the mainland's, and US, UK, and EU passports are on both visa-free lists.
Should I apply for a group tour visa instead? Only if a group tour suits your trip anyway. China's entry-exit regulations provide for group L visas for tour groups, but nothing official says they are easier to get, and you would travel with the group. Fixing the weak point in your own application is the more direct route.
What if my visa was rejected due to nationality or past travel? The consulate won't say, so you can't confirm that was the cause. What you can control: list the countries you visited in the past 12 months accurately, answer the questions about earlier China trips and visas, and explain anything unusual under "Anything else you want to declare".

What the COVA ‘Rejected and to be modified’ status actually means
Many travelers panic when their status flips to "Rejected and to be modified", but this is not a visa refusal. It means the online pre-review found something missing or not up to the requirements. Log back into your COVA account, read the embassy feedback, correct or add the materials, and resubmit without delay. Treat it as a request for revision, not a denial.
