If you are still searching for "China 144-hour visa-free cities," your information is out of date. On 17 December 2024 the National Immigration Administration replaced the 144-hour transit policy with a 240-hour (10-day) transit policy that covers all of the original 144-hour cities plus dozens of new ones. As of 2026 the old 144-hour rule no longer exists as a standalone program — but the city list is still useful because those are still the ports where transit travelers most commonly enter.

The Original 144-Hour Visa-Free Cities
The 144-hour (6-day) visa-free transit policy began in 2013 and expanded gradually through 2023. At its final version before December 2024, it covered 24 cities and regions across 19 provinces. Every one of these is now part of the expanded 240-hour policy.

Regional grouping rules under the old policy
Under the 144-hour rules, travelers could only move within the geographic region they entered. Enter through Beijing, visit Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei only. Enter through Shanghai, you were allowed to visit Jiangsu and Zhejiang but not Fujian. These regional boundaries were confusing and caused denials every week at immigration counters when travelers tried to take a HSR between regions.
What Changed on 17 December 2024
On 17 December 2024 the National Immigration Administration announced three simultaneous changes:
Duration: 144 hours (6 days) became 240 hours (10 days)

Geography: Regional silos were removed — travelers can now move between any of 24 eligible provinces and municipalities freely

Port count: The list of eligible entry ports was expanded from 37 to 60+

The practical impact: the 144-hour policy no longer exists as a separate option. Every traveler entering under the transit program now gets 240 hours and the expanded province access. There is no paperwork difference, no separate counter, and no extra fee.
Why the change happened
Chinese tourism fell to roughly 30% of 2019 levels during the pandemic and recovered slowly. The expansion was an explicit policy move to restart inbound tourism. Early data from the first quarter after the change showed a 60-80% increase in transit arrivals compared to the same period on the old 144-hour policy. The policy is now codified as standing law with no published expiry.

New seaports and land crossings
The expansion also added ports at Beihai (Guangxi), Haikou Meilan (Hainan), and Sanya Phoenix (Hainan), plus land border crossings including Pingxiang (Vietnam border) and Mohan (Laos border). Qingdao and Ningbo seaports accept cruise arrivals.
Provinces and regions now eligible for transit
Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Liaoning, Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian, Anhui, Shandong, Henan, Hubei, Hunan, Jiangxi, Guangdong, Guangxi, Hainan, Sichuan, Chongqing, Guizhou, Yunnan, Shanxi, Shaanxi, and Xinjiang.
Tibet, Qinghai, Inner Mongolia, Gansu, Ningxia, Jilin and Xinjiang are not on the list of 24, so they need a regular L visa. Heilongjiang is on the list but is one of the provinces that admits you to named cities only: the sole eligible port is Harbin Taiping International Airport and the permitted stay area is Harbin city, not the whole province.
Who Is Eligible in 2026
The 240-hour transit policy covers 55 passport nationalities. This is the same core list that was eligible for the old 144-hour rule, plus a handful of countries added in 2024 such as Norway and Romania.
Core eligible nationalities
North America: United States, Canada, Mexico

Europe: All 27 EU member states, UK, Switzerland, Norway, Iceland, Serbia, Albania, Belarus, Bosnia, Monaco, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Russia, Ukraine

Asia-Pacific: Japan, Singapore, South Korea, Brunei, Australia, New Zealand, UAE, Qatar

South America: Brazil, Argentina, Chile

If your passport is from a country not on the eligible list, the 240-hour transit is not available to you and you must apply for a standard L visa at a Chinese embassy or consulate.
The third-country rule still applies
Exactly like under 144-hour rules, you must be in transit from Country A through China to Country C, where A and C are different. Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan count as third destinations. A round-trip home from the same airport does not qualify.
If a Website Still Quotes 144 Hours
Dozens of travel blogs, tour operators, and even some embassy pages still mention "144-hour visa-free transit" in 2026. Here is how to interpret what you see:

Outdated sources are not dangerous in the sense that they under-promise — if you plan for the old 144-hour rule and only stay 6 days, you will not run into any problems. The danger is over-reading the old regional restrictions and skipping destinations you are actually allowed to visit.
How to Use the Current Rules Correctly
Step-by-step from booking to departure:
Verify passport eligibility — your country must be one of the 54 listed

Book a flight itinerary that transits through China — not a round trip from the same country

Ensure onward ticket is within 240 hours of arrival — 240 hours is exactly 10 days, counted from landing

Arrive at an eligible port — any of the 65 ports in the current list

Walk to the 240-hour / visa-free transit counter — it is signposted separately from the standard visa line

Present passport, onward ticket, and arrival card — be ready to say "Transit, 240 hours"

Pass immigration, collect the transit stamp — you can now move freely within 24 provinces

Register with police within 24 hours — most hotels do this automatically at check-in

Exit through an eligible port before 240 hours expires — can be the same or different port

Common Mistakes From Outdated Guides
Booking a round trip
Half of all transit denials at Shanghai Pudong come from travelers who booked New York-Shanghai-New York thinking it was a "transit" because they had a layover outside China. It is not. You must fly through China to a third destination.
Not counting hours correctly
240 hours is not "roughly 10 days" — it is exactly 240 hours from 00:00 of the day after landing, per the official interpretation. If you land at 14:30 on Monday, your 240-hour clock starts at 00:00 Tuesday and ends at 00:00 the following Friday (day 11). A ticket departing at 23:59 on the ending Thursday is still legal.
Trying to visit Tibet
Tibet Autonomous Region is not on the eligible province list and requires both a regular L visa and a Tibet Travel Permit. Several travelers each year attempt to fly Chengdu-Lhasa on a transit stamp and are turned away at Chengdu boarding. The Tibet restriction is firm; no exceptions.
Overstaying to see "one more thing"
Fines for overstaying are ¥500 per day up to ¥10,000 per offense, plus potential 3-5 year entry bans. Do not push it. If you need more time, exit to Hong Kong (which counts as a third country) and re-enter on a fresh 240-hour transit — as long as your next onward ticket again goes to a different third country.
Frequently Asked Questions
Is the 144-hour transit gone forever, or just suspended?
It was replaced, not suspended. The 144-hour rule no longer exists as an option. The 240-hour rule is strictly better for the traveler — longer duration, more ports, freer movement — so there is no reason to want the old rule back.
Why do so many websites still say "144-hour"?
Travel content is slow to update, and many tour operator pages are left untouched for years. Search engines also prioritize older, higher-authority pages over recent policy updates. Always verify transit rules within 30 days of your trip on the National Immigration Administration website or a recently updated source.
Can I apply for 240-hour transit at a Chinese consulate before flying?
No. The 240-hour transit is processed at the port of arrival, not in advance. If you want pre-approval for peace of mind, apply for a regular 10-year L visa ($140 for US citizens, valid for multi-entry).
Does the 240-hour policy apply to cruise passengers?
Yes, with a twist. Cruise passengers arriving at Qingdao, Ningbo, Shanghai, Sanya, or Haikou through a 240-hour eligible seaport get a 15-day visa-free shore stay instead of 240 hours, under a separate parallel policy. The 15-day stay is specifically for organized cruise tour groups and does not apply to independent cruise travelers.
What happens if my onward flight is cancelled?
Rebook to any third-country destination before your 240 hours expires. Keep the cancellation email as proof. If no flight is available, visit the local PSB Exit-Entry Administration immediately — they can issue an emergency extension in extraordinary cases, but it is discretionary and not guaranteed.
Can I enter on the old 144-hour policy if I only need 6 days?
No, because the 144-hour policy no longer exists as a separate option. Immigration processes every transit entry as a 240-hour stay regardless of your planned length. You are free to stay fewer days and leave early — the 240 hours is a maximum, not a required minimum. Just make sure your onward ticket leaves within the 240-hour window.
Which ports are best for first-time foreign visitors?
PEK (Beijing Capital), PVG (Shanghai Pudong), and CAN (Guangzhou Baiyun) are the three highest-volume 240-hour transit ports and have the most experienced immigration officers, longest English-speaking staff rosters, and clearest signage. If this is your first China trip, prefer these three over regional airports where transit volume is lower and officers may be less familiar with foreign documents.
Once you know the port, the next question is what a ten-day transit itinerary actually looks like when you can move between 24 provinces instead of one cluster — the 240-hour itinerary guide covers that, and the full visa-free picture separates transit from the 30-day scheme, which are the two lists people mix up most often.
If you are American, the transit route is the main way in and the rules are worth reading in your own terms — what Americans can and cannot do visa-free covers the onward-ticket requirement that causes most refusals.
