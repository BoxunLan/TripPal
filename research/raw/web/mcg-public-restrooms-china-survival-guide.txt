Public Restrooms in China 2026: Survival Guide for Travelers

A Canadian backpacker did a dance outside Tiananmen Square for almost 20 minutes last September because he could not find a public restroom. The line at the paid one near the National Museum was 40 minutes long. The nearest free one was inside Wangfujing APM mall, four blocks away. He made it. Barely. He also told me afterward that no one — not his visa agent, not Reddit, not any travel blog — had warned him that the bathroom thing in China is actually the most important thing to figure out on day one.
So let us figure it out.

Where to find public restrooms (the 2026 apps and tricks)
Every Chinese traveler I know uses one of three tools the moment they feel the first twinge. You should too.
Amap (高德地图) is the best free tool
Open Amap, tap the search bar, and type "厕所" (toilet) or just tap the "Nearby" category and pick "Public Toilet" (公共厕所). The app will show you every registered public restroom within 500 m, ranked by distance and with a star rating. In Beijing, Shanghai, Chengdu, Xi'an, Hangzhou and every provincial capital, coverage is near-total. Even small county towns have hundreds of mapped toilets now. If you only install one app before landing in China, make it Amap.
Baidu Maps works if you prefer English labels
Baidu has been slower than Amap on English, but their 2026 update finally translated most toilet POIs. The advantage: Baidu lists cleanliness tags pulled from user reviews ("clean," "has paper," "accessible"). Search "public toilet" or "restroom."
WeChat mini-programs for the very specific problem
Search "公厕地图" or "厕所查询" inside WeChat and you will find several mini-programs that do nothing except index public toilets with real-time reviews. "公共厕所地图" is the most popular. Useful in tourist-heavy areas where the official toilets are full.
Alipay Tour Pass and Amap both show paid vs free
When you tap a restroom pin in Amap, it tells you whether the toilet is free (免费), paid (收费), or "mall restroom" (商场). Free public toilets run by the city are the default. Paid ones cost ¥0.5-2 and usually mean someone is actively cleaning.
The McDonald's rule and the mall rule
You do not always need an official public restroom. You need a clean one. These two rules will carry you through every Chinese city.
The McDonald's / KFC / Starbucks rule
Any international chain with seating will let you use their bathroom without buying anything. This includes McDonald's, KFC, Pizza Hut, Starbucks, Burger King, Pret A Manger (in tier-1 cities), and Luckin Coffee (Chinese, but the rule still applies). Walk in, head directly to the back, find the restroom, done. No one will stop you. The staff do not care. In a pinch, these are your most reliable option anywhere in China.
Heytea (喜茶), Chagee (霸王茶姬) and other premium milk-tea chains in malls also have clean bathrooms, though you may need a 6-digit code from the receipt to unlock the door in some mall locations. If that happens, buy a ¥10 bottle of water at the cafe next door and ask for the bathroom code.
The mall rule
Any shopping mall in China — and there are thousands — has clean, free public restrooms on every floor. These are usually the best toilets in the neighborhood. Malls like Wangfujing APM (Beijing), Raffles City (Shanghai), Taikoo Li (Chengdu, Beijing), IFC, and SKP have bathrooms that would be considered high-end in most countries. If you see a mall, walk in. Security will not stop you. The bathrooms are usually near the elevators or behind the food court.
The gas station rule (highways)
On long-distance road trips or intercity buses, rest stops (服务区) have free public toilets every 40-60 km. Quality varies — Sinopec rest stops are generally cleaner than smaller private ones. They all have running water. Some have soap. Most do not have paper.
Squat vs western: how to actually use each
Older public toilets, village toilets, and many train station toilets are squat style. Newer malls, international chains, and 4-star+ hotels have western sit-down toilets. Tourist areas often have both. You will not always get a choice.
Using a squat toilet without drama
Pick a stall with a door that closes. Not all do.

Roll up your pant legs if you are wearing loose trousers. Splash back is real.

Face the hooded end of the porcelain slot. Some travelers face the wrong way on their first try.

Squat with your feet on the ridged footpads, not inside the slot.

Use your own toilet paper (see below). Drop it in the bin beside you unless a sign clearly says to flush.

Flush with the button or foot pedal. If there is no flush (rare but possible in rural areas), use the water bucket and ladle next to the stall.

Why you bin the toilet paper
Chinese plumbing often cannot handle toilet paper, especially in older buildings. The bins next to the toilet are for used paper. Signs that say "请勿将纸巾扔入马桶" mean "do not flush paper." Modern malls, chain hotels, and new tourist infrastructure can usually handle flushing. When in doubt, bin it.
If there is no door
Some rural toilets, park toilets, and older train station toilets still have squat stalls with half-walls and no doors. This is disappearing fast — the "Toilet Revolution" (厕所革命) campaign has replaced most of them since 2015 — but you can still find them. Keep looking. Walk to the next mall or McDonald's. In desperate cases, face the wall, do your business, and do not make eye contact with anyone.
Bring your own paper and wipes (seriously)
Toilet paper is the #1 thing first-time travelers forget. Roughly half of Chinese public toilets do not stock it. Even in malls, the dispenser may be empty by mid-afternoon.

Total damage: about ¥30-65, which you can assemble at any Watsons, Miniso, or 7-Eleven in under 10 minutes after landing. Buy it the first morning. Refill the tissue packs every few days.
Brand names that are easy to find
Vinda (维达) and Tempo (得宝) are the two best tissue brands in China. Both sell pocket packs at every convenience store. For wet wipes, Deoproce and Kleenex have wide distribution. Avoid buying toilet paper at tourist attraction gift shops — you will pay 3x the price.
Paid toilets, park toilets, and the ¥0-2 trap
Some older neighborhoods and a few tourist zones still run ¥0.5-2 paid toilets. These are usually the cleanest option in the area because someone is being paid to clean them. The attendant sits at a desk near the entrance, hands you a length of toilet paper, and takes your coin or scans your WeChat Pay QR code.
Pricing in 2026:
Standard city paid toilet: ¥1-2 (WeChat Pay or Alipay accepted)

Scenic-area paid toilet (Jiuzhaigou, Huangshan summit): ¥2-5

Train station paid toilet (rare now): ¥1

Tourist-trap paid toilet near Tiananmen, the Bund, or Xi'an Bell Tower: ¥2-5

City-run public toilets are free by law since the Toilet Revolution, so if someone is charging you at a bright clean "public" toilet, it is a private operator. The ¥1-2 is usually worth it for the cleanliness. Just have coins or WeChat Pay ready — they will not break a ¥100 note.
Hotel bathrooms, hostel bathrooms, and homestay surprises
Book a 3-star or higher hotel on Trip.com and you are guaranteed a western-style toilet, hot water, and toilet paper. Budget hostels (especially the Mingtown, YHA, and Hump chains) almost always have western toilets in the common bathrooms but may not stock unlimited paper. Bring your own backup roll.
Homestays and old hutong courtyard lodgings are the wild card. Some are beautifully renovated with proper bathrooms. Others share a squat toilet with the neighbors down the alley. Check the listing photos carefully on Trip.com or Airbnb — if there are no bathroom photos, ask before you book. A Dianping review mentioning "厕所比较基础" (basic bathroom) usually means squat and no paper.
Specific places to plan around
Train stations
Major high-speed rail stations (Beijing South, Shanghai Hongqiao, Guangzhou South) have clean, free restrooms with paper and sometimes even air conditioning. Older regular rail stations and small-city stations are squat-style, usually without paper. Before boarding a long-distance train, use the station bathroom. The on-board toilets in hard-seat carriages are rough.
Tourist attractions
The Forbidden City, Mutianyu Great Wall, Xi'an Terracotta Warriors, and Huangshan all have well-maintained public toilets near the main gates and scattered through the grounds. Inside the scenic zones, the toilets get rougher the farther you hike from the gate. Carry paper.
Parks and temples
Beijing's Temple of Heaven, Chengdu's People's Park, Shanghai's Yu Garden all have public toilets — usually free, usually squat, usually without paper. Bring your kit.
Restaurants
Nice sit-down restaurants in malls have clean restrooms. Hole-in-the-wall noodle shops often share a public toilet with the alley. If you are eating at a place with fewer than 6 tables and no printed menu, do not count on their bathroom. Go before you sit down.
Frequently asked questions
Do all public restrooms in China have toilet paper?
No. Assume paper is not provided unless you are in a new shopping mall, a 3-star+ hotel, or a McDonald's-style international chain. Carry Vinda or Tempo pocket tissues at all times. Refill your stash every few days at any convenience store. This is the single most useful habit for any China trip.
Are squat toilets hard to use?
No, but they take one round to get comfortable with. Face the hooded end of the porcelain, squat with your feet on the grippy pads, and keep your balance low. People with knee problems struggle more than anything else — if you have a bad knee, seek out mall or chain bathrooms with western toilets instead of park public toilets.
Are Chinese public restrooms clean?
It depends wildly on the location. Shopping mall and international chain bathrooms in tier-1 cities are excellent. City-run public toilets in tourist districts are usually clean but may lack paper. Older neighborhood toilets and rural toilets can be rough. The Amap rating system is surprisingly accurate — if a toilet has a 4+ star rating, it is worth walking to.
How do I say "where is the toilet" in Chinese?
"Cèsuǒ zài nǎlǐ?" (厕所在哪里?) works everywhere. If that pronunciation is too hard, point at your phone with Google Translate on "toilet" or show a staff member the characters 厕所. In polite company, "xǐshǒujiān" (洗手间) — "handwashing room" — is the more refined way to ask.
Is there an accessible or family restroom option?
Yes, increasingly. Most new malls, major train stations, and scenic areas have a "第三卫生间" (third-gender or family restroom) marked with a family icon. These are bigger, have changing tables, and are usually the cleanest option in the building. Search for "family restroom" or "第三卫生间" in Amap to find them.
Two places this matters more than in a city centre. On an overnight train , there is one squat and one Western toilet per carriage and the paper runs out by the second half of the night. And on a long road day in Xinjiang , the stops are checkpoints and rest areas rather than anywhere you would choose. Carry tissues either way.
