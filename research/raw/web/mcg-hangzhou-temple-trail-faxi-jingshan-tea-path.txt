Hangzhou Temple Trail: Faxi, Jingshan & the Matcha Tea Path

Most visitors to Hangzhou hit West Lake, queue 40 minutes for Lingyin Temple, eat a dragon well tea set, and leave. They miss the best walk in the city, which is not in the city at all. It's 45 minutes north, in Yuhang District, where a quiet 10-kilometer trail connects two mountain temples across tea fields and bamboo forest. The northern temple, Jingshan, is where the Japanese tea ceremony actually comes from. If you've ever whisked matcha at a kaiseki meal in Kyoto, the lineage runs back here.
This guide is for people who want one exceptional day outside the usual Hangzhou checklist. It assumes you're starting from the city center and returning the same day. No overnight needed, but the trail is quieter and the matcha tastings are better on weekdays.

The Quick Version: Why This Trail Matters
Jingshan (径山) is one of the "Five Mountains" of Chan (Zen) Buddhism in southern China. In the Song Dynasty, the temple developed a formal tea ceremony for guests, which Japanese monks Enni Ben'en and later Nanpo Jomyo studied here in the 1200s and carried back to Japan. That practice evolved into the Japanese tea ceremony you know. The mountain still grows matcha-style green tea, and the temple area has a modest but authentic Jingshan matcha revival underway.
Faxi Temple (法喜寺) is technically closer to downtown Hangzhou, built into the hillside above Lingyin. Many locals pair a Faxi morning visit with an afternoon trip out to Jingshan, which is what this trail does. You can also do the Jingshan Zen Culture Park (径山禅茶文化园) with the tea fields as a half-day if you skip Faxi.
Two notes before you plan:
The "temple trail" is not a single marked hiking trail like a US national park loop. It's a combination of paved temple access roads, stone steps, and forest paths maintained by the Zen Culture Park. You'll follow signs labeled 径山古道 (Jingshan Ancient Path).

Faxi Temple is inside Hangzhou's Xihu District, Jingshan Temple is in Yuhang District 35km north. You cannot walk between them, you drive or bus between them. The "trail" is the walk up and around Jingshan once you arrive.

This is, quite literally, where matcha comes from. In the 1200s Japanese Zen monks trained at Jingshan Temple and carried home its steamed-and-ground powdered tea (called moca or niancha, the direct ancestor of today's matcha), its tea utensils, and its monastic tea-ceremony etiquette. The Japanese tea ceremony, Cha-no-yu, grew out of those Jingshan rules. So walking up this mountain is not a generic temple hike; it is standing at the source of a ritual that 700 years later defines cafes from Kyoto to Brooklyn.
Getting There from Central Hangzhou
Option A: Taxi / DiDi Direct (Easiest)
DiDi from Wulin Square or Hangzhou East Station to Jingshan Zen Culture Park (径山禅茶文化园) costs ¥90-130 one-way and takes 50-70 minutes. This is what most visitors choose. Book the return through DiDi from Jingshan Town, but be aware that rural pickups can take 15-20 minutes.
Option B: Metro + Local Bus (Budget)
From Wulin Square, take Metro Line 1 to Linping (临平站), ¥8, 45 minutes. Transfer to local bus 428 or 508 toward Jingshan (径山), another 60-75 minutes, ¥6-10. The full door-to-door transit from downtown Hangzhou is 2 hours, and the bus only runs every 30-45 minutes. Not recommended unless you enjoy bus rides.
Option C: Rent a Car
Trip.com and local Hangzhou car rentals offer small sedans at ¥180-280 per day. You need an IDP and your home country license; international tourists typically skip this because of the Chinese driving rules. If you have a Chinese license or a resident friend, it's the most flexible option.
Combining with Faxi Temple in the Morning
If you want to see Faxi Temple first, start downtown, taxi to Faxi Temple (about ¥25, 15 minutes from West Lake). Spend 1-1.5 hours there, then taxi directly from Faxi to Jingshan Zen Culture Park for another ¥90-110, 55 minutes. Expect to spend around ¥180-220 on transport for the combined day trip.
The cleanest budget route is Hangzhou Metro Line 3 to Dongyue Station, then a local bus to the Tongqiao (Jingshan Temple) stop. By car or DiDi, navigate to the Dajingshan Tourist Distribution Center (大径山旅游集散中心), park there, and take the free shuttle bus up the mountain; alternatively drive to the Tongqiao trailhead, but its small lot fills early on weekends. A neat trick locals use: take the shuttle up, then walk the ancient trail down to Tongqiao, where a free shuttle loops you back to the distribution center, so you get the hike without the uphill grind.
Faxi Temple: Start the Day With Quiet Hillside Prayer
Faxi Temple is tucked into the hillside above the Lingyin scenic area. It's less famous than Lingyin, gets a fraction of the foot traffic, and the main hall has unusually large wooden beams salvaged from older temple reconstructions.
Entry: ¥10 for the temple, plus ¥5 for the scenic area if you enter from the main gate. Some local buses drop you near the temple gate directly and skip the scenic area fee.
What to see: the main Buddha hall, the arhat hall, and the small courtyard with ancient ginkgo trees. Monks sell incense packs for ¥10 if you want to light three sticks as a small offering. Photography is allowed outside the main hall but not inside.
Timing: budget 60-90 minutes. Early morning (7:30-9am) is best, the temple is genuinely empty and the air off the hillside is cool even in summer.
The matcha cafe: a small teahouse near the temple gate serves real Jingshan-style matcha brewed in bowls for ¥35-60. This is a good low-key taste before you go to the real thing at Jingshan.
Jingshan Zen Culture Park and the Ancient Trail
The park itself is free to enter. You pay separately for Jingshan Temple and for the tea field experiences. The park layout runs roughly:
Entrance and visitor center — parking, maps, ticket counter

Tea field walking paths — free, flat, photo spots

Jingshan Ancient Path (径山古道) — the 3km climb through bamboo forest

Jingshan Temple — at the top of the mountain, ¥15 entry

Tea ceremony hall — ¥80-150 for a guided tasting experience

Return via the ancient path or a shuttle — shuttle ¥10 if you're tired

The Ancient Path Hike
The climb up the ancient path runs roughly 3km one-way with 350m of elevation gain. Paved stone steps for most of the route, rougher dirt sections near the top. Difficulty is easy-to-moderate. Total hiking time: 60-90 minutes up, 45 minutes down. Spring bamboo shoots and autumn red maples are both worth timing a trip around.
The path is signed in Chinese and English, and there are rest pavilions roughly every 400m with benches and small water stalls. Bring at least 1L of water, more in summer. The climb is shaded almost entirely by bamboo and camphor trees, so summer hikes are cooler than you'd expect.
At the top, Jingshan Temple sits on a small plateau. The current temple is a 2011 reconstruction, the original was destroyed during the Cultural Revolution. Some stonework and a stele garden from the Song Dynasty survived. The newer main hall is a faithful Song-style rebuild with a tall wooden atrium.

Book the full ceremony on the Jingshan Zen Culture Park WeChat mini-program, search 径山禅茶 to find it. Weekend slots fill up 2-3 days in advance, weekdays usually have space same-day.
The matcha here is genuinely different from Japanese matcha. It's stone-ground from Jingshan-variety tea leaves with a slightly sweeter, less astringent profile than Uji matcha. Not better or worse, just distinct. Worth the trip even if you're matcha-skeptical.
Jingshan Temple (径山寺, full name Jingshan Wanshou Chan Temple) was founded in the Tang dynasty and rose to be ranked first among the Southern Song dynasty's Five Mountains and Ten Monasteries, the official top tier of Chan (Zen) Buddhism, earning it the title First Chan Monastery of the Southeast. Its most famous abbot, Wuzhun Shifan, drew Japanese monks across the sea: Enni Ben'en studied here from 1235 to 1241 and returned to Japan with tea seeds, the tea-drinking method, and a copy of the monastic Pure Rules, which he rewrote into his own temple's code and turned tea-drinking into formal ritual. Knowing this turns a 30-yuan temple visit into the actual room where a global tradition was exported.
There are two graded versions of the climb, both on smooth, well-laid stone steps through bamboo forest. The today's path (jindao) starts from Tongqiao village and runs about 2km to the temple, roughly one hour at an easy pace. The old path (gudao) — a thousand-year-old stone trail — starts from the former Guhuacheng Temple site and runs about 5km, the route pilgrims have used for over a thousand years (the Wuyue king and Song emperor Xiaozong both walked it). Combine an up-and-down with the surrounding tea terraces and you get the full 10km loop many local hikers do in 3 to 4 hours. None of it is technical; it is sustained stair-climbing, so it suits anyone reasonably fit but is not stroller-friendly.
You have three tiers of tea experience, all real and bookable on the ground. Simplest: a cup of Jingshan zen tea for about 38 yuan at Miaoxiang Hall (妙香堂) near the temple. Mid: a hands-on point-tea (diancha) session for about 60 yuan at the Jingshan village visitor center, where you whisk your own bowl Song-dynasty style. Full immersion: Mengshanjing (梦山径), phase one of the Jingshan Tea Culture Park in Pingyao Town, which opened in August 2024 and stages a recreation of the Jingshan Tea Banquet, the UNESCO-listed tea ritual, in Song-style architecture; reserve ahead by phone (0571-88503399).

Trail shoes or stable sneakers are fine. You do not need hiking boots. Flip-flops are a bad idea, the stone steps get slippery in the rain.
What to Eat Nearby
Jingshan Town at the base has a small cluster of farmhouse restaurants (农家乐) serving Hangzhou-style dishes. Expect ¥80-150 per person for a sit-down meal. Try:
Bamboo shoot stir-fry (春笋炒肉丝) — ¥40-60, seasonal in spring

Dragon Well shrimp (龙井虾仁) — ¥80, the classic Hangzhou dish

Jingshan tea-smoked fish (径山熏鱼) — ¥60, a local specialty

Vegetarian mountain platter (山珍拼盘) — ¥50, wild mushrooms and greens

Inside the Zen Culture Park, there's a small vegetarian restaurant run by the temple, lunch sets at ¥40-60 per person. Simple but clean, aligned with Buddhist vegetarian tradition.
Sample Full Day Itinerary
8:00am — Depart Hangzhou hotel by taxi to Faxi Temple 8:30am — Arrive Faxi, walk the grounds, light incense, short meditation 9:45am — Taxi from Faxi to Jingshan Zen Culture Park 10:45am — Arrive Jingshan, enter park, walk tea fields and photograph 11:30am — Begin ancient path climb to Jingshan Temple 12:45pm — Arrive temple, tour the main hall and stele garden 1:15pm — Temple vegetarian lunch (¥40 set) or descend for farmhouse meal 2:30pm — Tea ceremony at the Zen Culture Park tea hall 4:00pm — Descend to visitor center, shuttle or walk 4:45pm — DiDi back to central Hangzhou 6:00pm — Arrive downtown Hangzhou
Adjust pace to taste. If you skip Faxi, start later at Jingshan (9:30am departure is fine) and you'll have more time for the tea ceremony.
Weekday vs. Weekend
On weekdays, the entire trail feels private. You'll see maybe 30-50 other hikers across the whole day. On Chinese weekends and public holidays, the park draws local Hangzhou day-trippers and the tea ceremony slots book out. If your schedule allows, go Monday through Thursday. Avoid Chinese New Year week, Labor Day (early May), and National Day (October 1-7 Golden Week).
Seasonal Notes: When the Trail Actually Shines
Season changes the character of this trail more than you'd expect. Each month offers something different, and a few months are worth planning a trip around.
Spring (March-May)
This is the peak window. Late March brings the first tea harvest (头采, "first pick"), and the Jingshan tea fields are dotted with pickers in traditional wide-brimmed hats. New bamboo shoots break through the soil along the ancient path, and you'll see farmhouses selling freshly dug shoots for ¥15-25 per kilogram. Temperatures run 15-22°C, clouds clear by mid-morning, and the forest smells of wet earth and ferns. Late April is when most Chinese tea enthusiasts book tea ceremony slots, so reserve 5-7 days ahead.
Summer (June-August)
Hot and humid by Chinese standards, 28-36°C with afternoon thunderstorms. Mornings are bearable, afternoons brutal. The bamboo canopy provides real shade on the ancient path, so the climb stays cooler than exposed Hangzhou streets. Mosquitoes are active, carry repellent. The temple courtyards run cooler than outside due to the stone construction and elevated location. Avoid weekends, the park draws Shanghai day-trippers escaping the lowland heat. Early-morning starts (before 8am at the trailhead) make the difference between comfortable and miserable.
Autumn (September-November)
Second peak window. September brings the autumn tea harvest, smaller than spring but considered finer by some tea masters. The bamboo forest turns a richer green and the camphor trees start dropping yellow leaves onto the stone steps. Late October and early November bring the red maples near the temple summit. Weather is stable, 14-22°C, dry afternoons, clear mountain views. No rain risk. If you can only pick one season, come in late October.
Winter (December-February)
Quiet and cold, 3-10°C, occasional light snow in January. Some temple buildings close for repairs. The tea fields go dormant and the ceremony hall runs a reduced schedule. Fewer visitors means the trail feels genuinely abandoned, which some travelers love. Wear layers and waterproof shoes, the stone steps hold ice in shaded sections after a rain. Winter light is beautiful for photography, hard shadows on the bamboo make striking compositions.
Practical Tips for First-Time Visitors
Bring cash in small denominations. Temple incense booths, roadside farmhouses, and small tea sellers often prefer cash to mobile pay despite the nationwide QR-code economy. ¥100-200 in ¥10 and ¥20 notes covers most needs.

Download Amap (高德地图) with the park name preloaded. Trail branches are not always well marked, and Google Maps does not work reliably here. Amap has the ancient path drawn accurately.

The cell signal is spotty inside the bamboo forest. Text your hotel or host before you start the climb, not mid-trail.

Carry your passport. Some temples require passport ID for foreign visitors to enter, though Faxi and Jingshan are currently relaxed about this.

Do not bring large backpacks into temple halls. Most temples require you to leave bags at the entrance or carry them on your front, not your back, as a mark of respect.

No meat picnics. Eating meat on temple grounds is considered disrespectful. Save your farmhouse lunch for the base of the mountain.

Rain gear. Hangzhou spring showers come fast. A ¥20 plastic poncho from any 7-Eleven keeps you moving when the sky opens up.

Jingshan is the only one on this list that combines hiking, temples, and tea at a low crowd density. If you have three days in Hangzhou and want each day to feel different, this slots between a West Lake day and a Longjing tea day without overlap.
One thing to clear up first, because most English guides blur it: Faxi Temple and Jingshan are two different places about 50km apart. Faxi Temple (法喜寺, also called Shangtianzhu) is the wisteria-and-prayer hillside temple right by West Lake and Lingyin, a 15-minute shuttle from Lingyin's exit. Jingshan (径山) is a forested mountain in Yuhang District, roughly a 40-minute to one-hour drive northwest of the city center. They are not on the same trail and cannot be walked between in a day. If matcha and the ancient temple hike are what you came for, that is all Jingshan, and that is what this trail guide is really about. Pair Faxi only if you want a separate, easy half-day near West Lake on a different day.
Spring is the one window worth planning around. The pre-Qingming harvest (late March to early April) is when Jingshan tea is picked, and the hillsides fill with pickers; this is also when the famous Jingshan tea banquet and tea-culture events peak. The trade-off is that this is peak local-tourist season, so go on a weekday.
FAQ
Is the Jingshan temple trail suitable for beginners?
Yes. The climb has about 350m of elevation gain spread over 3km, which is roughly equivalent to walking up 90 flights of stairs. Most reasonably fit travelers do it comfortably in 60-90 minutes with rest stops. There are no technical sections, ropes, or scrambling.
Can I see both Faxi and Jingshan in one day?
Yes, if you start early. Faxi in the morning (7:30-9:30am), then taxi to Jingshan for a late morning climb and early afternoon tea ceremony. It's a full day with about 60 minutes of transit between the two. Rushing is optional, the trip works at a slower pace by skipping one or the other.
How does Jingshan matcha differ from Japanese matcha?
Jingshan matcha uses local tea cultivars ground on stone mills in a revival of the Song Dynasty tradition. The flavor is slightly sweeter and less umami-heavy than Uji matcha, with more grassy notes. The preparation ceremony at Jingshan predates the Japanese tea ceremony and is the historical source. Both are worth trying for comparison.
Is there a cable car or shuttle if I can't climb?
Yes. An electric shuttle runs from the visitor center to Jingshan Temple for ¥10 one-way, ¥18 round trip. This lets you skip the ancient path entirely. The shuttle runs every 20-30 minutes during park hours.
What's the best time of year to visit?
April and late October are peak. April has fresh bamboo shoots, mild weather, and new tea leaves being picked. Late October has red maples, cool air, and clear skies. March and November are also excellent. Avoid July-August (hot, humid) and January-February (cold, some temple buildings may be closed for restoration).
Is Jingshan really where matcha was invented? The leaf-grinding powdered tea was Chinese and pre-dated Japan, but the specific lineage that became Japanese matcha and the tea ceremony was carried directly from Jingshan Temple by monks in the 1200s, so Japan itself credits Jingshan as a source of its tea ceremony. Calling it matcha's birthplace is fair shorthand, not marketing.
Did the tea sage Lu Yu have a connection here? Yes, and almost no English guide mentions it. Lu Yu, author of The Classic of Tea, the world's first book devoted to tea, lived in seclusion near Jingshan at Shuangxi around 760 AD, and a spring there is still called Lu Yu Spring after him. So Jingshan touches both the Chinese origin of tea writing and the export of tea ritual to Japan.
If the tea is what draws you here rather than the walk, the deeper version of this trip is in Yunnan, where the leaf actually comes from — the Yunnan tea guide covers the Kunming wholesale markets and why Lijiang's old-town shops are the wrong place to buy. Closer to hand, Shaoxing rice wine is the other thing worth learning to order properly around Hangzhou, and Suzhou is 25 minutes away by train .
